For weeks, I felt paralyzed by the scale of it all. Eight million tons of plastic enter the ocean 
every year. The amount is jaw -dropping , equivalent to dumping a garbage truck into the sea 
every minute. What could any individual action possibly accomplish against that tide? 
In Week 8 of our course, as we explored how the sea , which covers over 71% of Earth’s 
surface and the prosperity of all life, regulates global climate while absorbing the b urden of 
human activity, I stood on the metaphorical shoreline of our collective future. A single 
photograph of a turtle entangled in a ghost net , shown in lecture, etched itself into my 
mind: an animal even more ancient than dinosaurs , killed by our selfishness and 
carelessness. That image crystallized the tension: the ocean gives us lif e and stability, yet 
we treat it as a sink for our waste. 
Across the semester, the evidence accumulated like drifting microplastics. We examined 
how the ocean acts as Earth’s primary thermostat, absorbing vast amounts of heat and 
carbon dioxide, yet plastic pollution is the killer of this quality . Every year, millions of tons of 
plastic enter marine ecosystems and fragment into microplastics that disrupt the food web 
from plankton to tuna to humans. Traditional Western science often frames the ocean as a 
resource to be quantified and exploited, overlooking the rel ational, local-based knowledge 
of Pacific Islanders who have lived with and protected these waters for millennia. But I’ve 
learnt to recognize that indigenous communities in low -lying atolls are not just victims of 
rising seas and plastic -choked fisheries, but holders of solutions rooted in reciprocity with 
the marine world. 
Furthermore, when we connected global ocean crises to Hong Kong’s 2100 horizon , I 
realized that the problem was closer than I expected. As a coastal city reliant on maritime 
trade and vulnerable to sea -level rise, Hong Kong mirrors the ocean’s plight: our Victoria 
Harbour and surrounding waters already show signs of plastic accumulation from dense 
urban runoff and shipping. A study from the Duke University Press in 2021 revealed how 
global supply chains flood the seas with single -use packaging. Yet, one important thing I 
learnt from this course is that we should look for direction rather than despair. We explored 
how decolonized approaches could reshape responses: community -led monitoring in the 
Pacific that combines traditional ecological knowledge with modern tools . In Hong Kong’s 
context, this could mean policies that hold corporations accountable for packaging while 
empowering local initiatives to clean and restore our own marine environments. 
It is ironic, the very system that gives us life has been turned into a waste storage. Yet within 
that same story lies resilience and the real possibility of redemption. The future is not yet 
sealed. 

Instead, clear and hopeful pathways are within our reach . For starters, w e can hold 
companies accountable through extended producer responsibility laws, requiring 
manufacturers to reduce their packaging. Furthermore, we can enhance technologies that 
intercept plastic in rivers before it reaches the sea while supporting community -led 
cleanups like giving them donations. And here in Hong Kon g, we can integrate decolonized 
science into both education and policy, training the next generation to combine rigorous 
data with traditional wisdom for ocean governance that actually works. 
These steps are concrete. Pacific nations like Germany are s upporting “blue economy” 
models that significantly cut down on single -use plastic with the aforementioned methods . 
What remains is the will to scale them through personal habit shifts like refusing single -use 
items, supporting refill infrastructure and collective advocacy of pushing for bolder climate 
policies that address ocean health as central to Hong Kong 2100. 
Critics may argue that the above remedies like deposit-return schemes and blue economy 
frameworks may fail to achieve true transformation. Reasoning that d eposit-return systems 
may encourage single -use consumption through financial incentives and Pacific blue 
economy models sometimes prioritize growth over deep indigenous stewardship . 
That being said, these limitations overlook their demonstrated power when properly 
designed. Germany’s scheme has achieved a consistent 98% return rate, slashing litter and 
enabling high -quality closed -loop recycling far beyond conventional systems. Fiji’s Blue 
Economy Framework was developed through inclusive consultations with customary 
owners, explicitly integrating traditional knowledge as a core foundation. Paired with 
production caps and community governance, these tools provide tangible, decolonized 
pathways progress towards ocean recover y. 
Finally, the semester taught me that knowledge without narrative remains shallow, and 
narrative without action is hollow. I leave with a commitment: to advocate for ocean 
solutions that are scientific and actionable. The waves are still rising , but so can our 
response.
