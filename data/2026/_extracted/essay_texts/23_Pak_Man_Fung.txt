Should Hong Kong mandate a sustainable seafood labelling scheme to reduce overfishing caused by overconsumption? In the 1980s, Sai Kung sea-urchin diver Ng Wai-keung could fill buckets in one morning and earn thousands of dollars daily. By the late 1990s, the same reef produced almost nothing. Overfishing had stripped away the algae his urchins depended on, forcing him to quit fishing and ending a four-generation livelihood (China Daily, 2017). His story raises an urgent question for Hong Kong: should the city mandate sustainable seafood labels to reduce overfishing driven by overconsumption? Hong Kong’s seafood appetite is unusually high. Using FAO data, a government-commissioned study estimated seafood consumption at 77.5 kilograms per person per year, placing Hong Kong second in Asia and sixth globally (Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation Department [AFCD], 2011). WWF-Hong Kong later estimated that in 2017 each resident still consumed 66.5 kilograms of seafood, more than three times the world average (WWF-Hong Kong, 2017). Local waters unfortunately cannot keep up with the surging demand. Assessments of 17 common marine fish species found 12 to be heavily over-exploited and the other five fully exploited, while the biomass of bottom-dwelling fish has fallen by roughly 90 percent since the 1970s (Lee et al., 2025). This suggests that overfishing in the city is mainly caused by overconsumption. Globally, overfishing is a serious problem that 

requires urgent action, as it depletes key species, weakens ecosystem balance, and damages marine food webs (NOAA, 2024). For that reason, I propose Hong Kong should mandate a sustainable seafood labelling scheme, since the current market conceals ecological costs from consumers. Most diners cannot know whether a fish comes from a depleted fishery or a less destructive capturing method. As such, overfishing remains invisible when people make choices and purchases. A mandatory scheme would enable this unrevealed information visible. The government can build on WWF-Hong Kong’s existing traffic-light Seafood Guide, and require major supermarkets, chain restaurants, and seafood retailers to display labels consisting of four details on every seafood item: “species name”, “origin”, “fishing method”, and a “coloured rating” (green for “better choice”, yellow for “use less”, and red for “avoid”). Such labels are easy to understand, while the detailed explanation can be accessed through WWF-HK or government websites. I believe this approach is far more effective than simply awareness campaigns, as it serves to fill a real information gap. Despite voluntary guides already existing, they depend on consumers searching for them voluntarily in advance, unlike the mandatory labels which reach consumers at the exact moment of purchase. Research on seafood ecolabels found that clear and credible labels can shift market share toward certified products and can sometimes support modest price premiums (Roheim et al., 2011). In 

Hong Kong, where seafood is often bought rapidly in wet markets or ordered without much thought in restaurants, that matters a lot. Although the labels cannot solve overfishing on their own, they can change the demand pattern significantly, which directly targets Hong Kong’s major cause for overfishing. A possible criticism to the proposed labeling scheme may be the disproportionate burden on local small businesses. This concern is reasonable, and hence the policy should be phased in. Large supermarket chains and franchised restaurant groups can comply first, owing to their centralised procurement systems and greater capacity to absorb administrative costs. Smaller wet-market stalls could follow afterwards at later phases with governmental support. The proposed labelling scheme’s benefits go far beyond stickers on menus. For consumers, it provides clear and immediate information that makes sustainable choices easier at the moment of purchase. For retailers and importers, it creates pressure to move away from red-rated species and source more responsibly, especially when reputation and consumer trust are at stake. Over time, this could largely reduce demand for the most vulnerable fish, ease pressure on depleted stocks, and support the recovery of stressed fisheries. It may also encourage a broader cultural shift in Hong Kong, making seafood sustainability part of ordinary consumption rather than a niche environmental concern. 

Readers need not wait for legislation. They can open WWF-Hong Kong’s Seafood Guide today, screenshot the red list on their phones, and avoid those species at their next hotpot, sushi restaurant, or family seafood dinner. If more and more consumers do this, formal labelling will become much easier to justify. The Hong Kong government should legislate a mandatory sustainable seafood labelling scheme now. Without clear labels, overfishing stays hidden behind everyday consumption. A science-based law would not solve the crisis overnight, but it can at least expose the truth.
