Singapore Breathes, Hong Kong Suffocates: 
Mandating Green Facades to Break the Air -Conditioning Trap 
 
On a July night in Kennedy Town, Kenneth Fan sat waiting for a spot to sleep in a 
community cooling centre. His apartment was unbearable , concrete walls still radiating the day’s 
heat at midnight , but running the air conditioner all night would wreck his family’s budget. Across 
Hong Kong, millions face the same impossible choice every summer, trapped in a cycle where the 
machines meant to cool them heat the city . 
Hong Kong’s relationship with air conditioning has become a public health crisis disguised 
as a comfort. Air conditioners consume 31% of the city’s total electricity, and electricity generation 
accounts for over 60% of its greenhouse gas emissions (HKUST D ivision of Public Policy, 2022). 
This energy -intensive cooling feeds the urban heat island effect: dense concrete towers absorb 
solar radiation by day and radiate it back at night, raising urban temperatures up to 5°C above 
surrounding rural areas (South C hina Morning Post, 2023). Between 2022 and 2024, Hong Kong 
recorded up to 56 “hot nights” annually , nights where temperatures never drop below 28°C 
(Earth.Org, 2025). By 2100, projections suggest 150 such nights per year. The hotter the city gets, 
the harder its air conditioners work; the harder they work, the hotter it gets. 
Hong Kong should mandate green facades, vertical greenery systems integrated into 
building exteriors -on all new developments and major retrofits, adopting the regulatory model that 
Singapore has refined since 2005. The solution is not speculative; it is proven, scalable, and already 
transforming a comparable Asian city. 
Singapore’s Building and Construction Authority requires all new buildings to meet 
minimum energy performance standards under the Green Mark scheme, with mandatory greenery 

replacement through its Landscaping for Urban Spaces and High -rises programme (National 
Climate Change Secretariat, Singapore, 2025). The Skyrise Greenery Incentive Scheme funds up 
to 50% of installation costs for rooftop and vertical greenery, having supp orted over 300 hectares 
of urban greening since 2009. The results are tangible: peer -reviewed research demonstrates that 
green facades reduce indoor temperatures by up to 3 -4°C and cut cooling energy loads by 10 - 31% 
through shading, evapotranspiration, an d thermal insulation (Wong et al., 2020). Singapore now 
aims to green 80% of its buildings by 2030. 
Hong Kong could implement this in three steps. First, amend the Building Energy Codes 
to require vertical greenery coverage on at least 30% of exposed fa cades for all new developments 
above ten storeys . Second, establish a Skyrise Greenery Fund modelled on Singapore’s scheme, 
subsidising 50% of retrofitting costs for existing buildings, prioritising public housing estates 
where heat vulnerability is greatest. Third, integrate green facade performance me trics into the 
existing BEAM Plus green building certif ication, creating market incentives for developers to 
exceed minimum standards. The estimated cost is offset by reduced electricity bills: if green 
facades cut cooling loads by even 15% across Hong Kong’s commercial building stock, the annual 
electricity s avings would be substantial, given that air conditioning alone consumes over 49 
terajoules citywide (EMSD, 2023). 
The beneficiaries are immediate and widespread. Elderly residents in subdivided flats, most 
vulnerable to heat -related hospitalisation , which rises 4.5% per degree above 29°C (Legislative 
Council of Hong Kong, 2021) , would gain cooler indoor environments without the crushing burden 
of higher electricity bills. Building owners would see reduced operating costs. The city as a whole 
would lower its carbon footprint while reversing the concrete -canyon aesthetics that make Hong 
Kong’s streetscapes feel oppressiv e in summer. 

The cost of inaction is equally quantifiable. On current trajectories, the projected shift from 
56 to 150 hot nights annually would triple heat -related hospitalisations among the elderly, push air 
conditioning from 31% toward a majority share of electricit y demand, and lock in a feedback loop 
where only wealthier residents can afford to escape the heat they collectively generate. Every year 
of delay raises both the retrofitting bill and the public health toll. 
The next step is within reach for any reader: write to your District Council member and 
demand that green facade requirements be included in Hong Kong’s next Climate Action Plan 
review. One email, one specific ask. The infrastructure exists; the science is settled; the regulatory 
model is proven. What is missing is political will , and that begins with public pressure. 
Kenneth Fan should not have to choose between sleep and solvency. No city that calls itself 
world -class can cool its residents by heating the planet. Singapore breathes because it decided to. 
Hong Kong can too , but only if it chooses green walls over grey ones.
