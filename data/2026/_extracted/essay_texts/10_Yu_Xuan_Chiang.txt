“As the Sea Dies, We Die”: Saving Hong Kong’s Oceans Through Community -Led 
Marine Protected Areas and Sustainable Fishing 
In 2024, a fisherman in Sai Kung pulled up his nets and found more plastic bags and jellyfish 
than grouper. His grandfather once filled the same boat with fish in a single morning. Now 
some days he returns with almost nothing. The water is too warm, the re efs are bleaching 
white, and the sea that fed generations is dying. As the sea dies, we die. 
Hong Kong’s coastal waters are in a critical crisis. Local coral communities suffered large -
scale bleaching events in both 2022 and 2024 due to marine heatwaves. Coral bleaching was 
again recorded at seven sites in summer 2025 (Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation 
Department, Hong Kong Reef Check 2025 ). At the same time, up to 3.2 billion microplastic 
pieces (“Hong Kong Drains Pour Billions of Pieces of Microplastics into the Sea Every 
Day.”, CUHK 2023 ) flow from the city’s storm drains into the sea ever y day, and fish stocks 
have collapsed under decades of overfishing. And the worst of all, o nly about 5 –6% of Hong 
Kong’s territorial waters are currently protected as Marine Protected Areas ( “Designated 
Marine Parks” , Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation Department ). Hong Kong’s fishing 
industry still supports over 10,000 local fishermen and generates hundreds of millions in 
annual value, yet decades of overfishing have pushed iconic species like the Hong Kong 
grouper toward local extinction. On a globa l scale, oceans are losing coral reefs and fish 
faster than they can recover. Coral reefs supports 25% of all marine life, have lost half their 
cover since 1950 with climate -driven heatwaves accelerating the collapse. Now, the ocean 
problem had become a s ystems -level crisis t hat is already directly impacting various sectors 
of our society, for instance our food security, coastal protection, and our city’s long -term 
resilience is all at risk. 
At first glance, t he problem may sound too overwhelming to tackle , but the solution might be 
simpler than you think. I n the context of Hong Kong, simply expand ing MPAs with 
community -supported sustainable fishing has already been proven effective in preserving fish 
population s. Currently H ong Kong has eight marine parks and one marine reserve covering 
roughly 8,517 hectares (“Designated Marine Parks” , Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation 
Department ), however, it only accounts for less than 5% of HK’s coastal region . Another 
example, s cientists in WWF -Hong Kong have identified seven priority sites including Sai 
Kung, Hoi Ha Wan, Pak Nai, and other s that urgently need stronger protection. These 
protection include establishing new no -take zones, prohibiting destructive fishing and run 
active restoration of habitats and nature systems. Coral nurseries, mangrove planting and 
ghost -net removals are all necessary and effective actions to make. Looking at successful 
accounts, WWF’s Reviving Our Corals project has already planted hundreds of coral 
fragments with 90% survival rates (“Reviving Our Corals” , WWF Hong Kong 2025 ), well-
managed no take zones see substantial fish population recovery, two - to five -fold within 
merel y 5 years (“Recommendations for Pak Nai Marine Protected Area ”, The Nature 
Conservancy 2025 ). These are already proven cases of progress . 
Moreover, HK’s traditional fishermen do not lose their living, instead they gain from 
healthier fish stock s while the city gains natural typhoon barriers from restored mangroves 
and reefs. All fishermen need to do is to receive some training, better gear, and fair prices for 
eco-friendly catch through models like the WWF Seafood Guide. Eco-protection should be 
treated as smart management that fuses sustainable ideals with conventional industries, 
systems thinking in action, conservation and people working together instead of putting 
people out of jobs. The key to success is to make everyone a shareholder , make t he positive 

or negative impact visible and tangible, aligning targeted protection and market incentives to 
create win -win outcomes. Fishermen can keep their identity and income , Hong Kong families 
receive healthier local seafood. All of humanity inherit cleaner beaches, richer biodiversity, 
and a stronger defence against climate change for the generations to come. 
Citizens can contribute meaningfully to ocean conservation by consulting the WWF -Hong 
Kong Sustainable Seafood Guide to select green -listed species and by participating in local 
restoration efforts such as mangrove planting and beach clean -ups. When scaled , these 
actions generate market pressure and public momentum for stronger government policies and 
expanded marine protected areas. Having thi s integrated approach exemplifies a system atic 
thinking and synthesises insights from food systems, economics, and urban sustainabilit y, 
demonstrating that conservation can deliver co -benefits for biodiversity and livelihoods rather 
than trade -offs. 
As the sea dies, we die. But when we choose to protect it, we give life back to Hong Kong’s 
waters , and to ourselves. The choice is ours, right now .
