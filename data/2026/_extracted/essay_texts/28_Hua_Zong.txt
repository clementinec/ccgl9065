In a Hong Kong supermarket, a shopper picks up a neatly packaged beef imported from Brazil. What she cannot see is that, in São Paulo state, farmers suffering from drought desperately watch their rivers shrink. Where does their water go? Ask the cattle. Every kilogram of beef consumed in Hong Kong carries an invisible 15455 litres of water, enough to fill a small swimming pool, mostly for growing feed crops. while the average Hong Kong resident’s food-related water footprint stands at 4727 litres per person per day, almost double the Mainland Chinese average, due to high consumption of animal products (Hong Kong Water Supplies Department, 2024; Vanham et al., 2019).

Hong Kong imports 95% of its food, meaning the city's food consumption is almost entirely embedded in goods produced elsewhere, yet none of this hidden water cost appears on the label (EEHB, 2011). Globally, agriculture accounts for over 70% of freshwater withdrawals worldwide (FAO, 2020). Hong Kong's diet-driven demand quietly amplifies this pressure while remaining entirely invisible at the point of sale.

This invisibility is not inevitable—it is a choice. Hong Kong should introduce a phased, mandatory water footprint label on imported food products. This is a standardized panel disclosing litres of water consumed per 100 grams, similar in design to existing nutritional information, combined with a public education campaign and incentives for lower-water proteins. The methodology already exists. The Water Footprint Network provides internationally recognized accounting standards applicable across food categories (Hoekstra et al., 2011). 

The behavioral aspect is strong. Building on Thaler and Sunstein's nudge framework, research consistently finds that visible, standardized labels shift purchasing choice without restricting them. One controlled study found nutritional labels produced a statistically significant 7% reduction in high-calorie purchases (Freedman & Brochado, 2010). Critically, simplicity matters that color-tiered labels in the front of the pack outperform complex numerical data in changing purchase intention (Graça et al., 2019). A green-to-red tier alongside a single number would allow shoppers to grasp relative water intensity in seconds. This makes the recognition process simple, shifting consumer preference for a large number of water intensive food products, creating real market pressure on the most water-intensive imports, and will then result in reduced water usage in production.

The rollout need not be abrupt. A realistic Hong Kong model would begin with a voluntary pilot in Year 1, engaging major supermarket chains to trial water footprint disclosure on red meat, dairy, and rice. Mandatory coverage of all imported food and packaged goods would follow in Year 3, enforced through the Food and Environmental Hygiene Department, which already administers Hong Kong's nutrition labelling regulations. This phased logic mirrors Australia's Water Efficiency Labelling and Standards scheme, which had already saved over 300 megalitres of water daily and delivering household bill savings of one billion Australian dollars per year (Waterwise, 2021).

If successful, this initiative would significantly lower Hong Kong’s overall virtual water footprint, ease pressure on global water-stressed regions, and set a model for other import-dependent cities. Hong Kong families, students, and future generations would benefit from greater awareness and healthier eating patterns. Restaurants and canteens could innovate with attractive low-water dishes, potentially creating new market opportunities.

However, Critics will argue that a sharp drop in beef demand could harm beef producers’ livelihoods. Many farmers and workers in the supply chain depend on stable export markets; sudden shifts risk unemployment and economic instability in rural communities. To mitigate this, transition measures such as financial support, technical assistance for sustainable farming, and gradual implementation are essential. Agricultural subsidies should redirect away from high-water industrial beef production toward diversified, so together with the labelling policy, agroecological farming can work simultaneously on both demand and supply side.

WWF-Hong Kong has already called for labelling systems empowering consumers to make sustainable food choices (WWF-Hong Kong, 2023). You can add your voice by writing to the Food and Environmental Hygiene Department on food safety labelling. Or, you can simply track your own meals for three days and calculate the virtual water used (try the HK Water Footprint Calculator). Then, replace one beef dish with a lower-impact option. Small consistent choices, multiplied across 7.5 million residents, create real impact. The label is not a ban—it is a mirror. 

To recapitulate, Hong Kong’s appetite for beef hides a massive water debt that affects the world. By making it visible and supporting a fair transition for producers, we can enjoy food responsibly while protecting shared resources. The choice is on our plates.
