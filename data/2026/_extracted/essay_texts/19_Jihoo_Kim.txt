 Clearing the Air: A Reflection on Hong Kong's Air Pollution Crisis 
Word: 702 
 
Agnes has run a newspaper stand in Central for twenty years, breathing the heavy 
exhaust generated by buses and ferries. “I’m used to it,” she told reporters in 2025 (HK2050 Is 
Now, 2025). Her statement is striking not for its persistence, but for what it reveals about 
normalization. In the same year, air pollution was responsible for 3,508 d eaths in Hong Kong 
and imposed an economic burden of HK$42 billion which is not from a disaster, but from 
breathing (Clean Air Network [CAN], 2025). 
 
Working on my collage about Hong Kong’s air crisis compelled me to confront a number I had 
previously regarded as abstract. In 2024, roadside nitrogen dioxide levels reached 65 µg/m³ 
which is 550% above the World Health Organization guideline, while fine p articulate matter 
exceeded safe limits by three times (CAN, 2025). These are not distant statistics. They are the air 
outside the classroom, the bus stop, and the wet market. Globally, air pollution accounts for 
approximately seven million deaths annually (Lau et al., 2023). What troubles me most is not the 
scale of the problem but how thoroughly Hong Kong has learned to accept it. 
 
 The solution I believe in is one that remains underemphasized in local climate discourse: 
prioritizing the replacement of diesel engines in public and commercial transport. I am proposing 
that the mandatory and accelerated electrification of the entire public transports such as buses, 
taxies, minibuses, and more should be enforced through binding legal tim elines rather than

 aspirational roadmaps. This approach is not radical, instead it represents a necessary realignment 
of policy priorities. 
 
Interestingly, current policy attention mostly favors private vehicle electrification, as 
they posses greater political visibility. But private cars sit in garages for most of the day. A diesel 
bus does not. It runs twelve to sixteen hours daily through th e decent corridors in one of the most 
densely populated cities on earth. Although road transport contributes approximately 20% of 
Hong Kong’s total greenhouse gas emissions, commercial vehicles account for the majority of 
direct and street -level pollution exposure (Environment Bureau, 2021). Individuals like Agnes 
are not affected by emissions, they are exposed to the exhaust of vehicles throughout the day. 
The effective solution has to be designed around patterns of actual exposure, not ownership. 
 
 The proof that this work s is thirty minutes away by MTR, Shenzhen. They achieved a 
significant reduction in average air pollution levels by fully electrifying their public transport in 
2019 (HK2050 Is Now, 2025). Hong Kong already established a policy framework, including the 
2021 Electric Vehicle Roadmap and subsequent updates in 2024 and 2026 (Environment and 
Ecology Bureau [EEB], 2021, 2026). But in late 2024, only 0.86% of buses and 0.17% of 
minibuses were gas -free (Business Environment Council, 2025). Three binding steps would turn 
it into an achievable plan. First, by implementing electric -only procurement for franchised buses 
like Citybus by 2027. Second, by establishing legall y binding electrification targets for 
minibuses by 2030. Lastly, by introducing transition subsidies funded through diesel tax 
revenues to support operators facing financial constraints.

 Research indicates that residents in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas face 
significantly higher mortality risks from air pollution than those in wealthier populations (Wong 
et al., 2008). Outdoor workers, elderly residents residing near major roads, and children in older 
school buildings are exposed to the risks in high chance. These are the people who cannot opt out 
of environmental risk. Thus, cleaning th e shared air is the only intervention that reaches all of 
them at once. Importantly, there is a clear avenue for civic engagement. The Legislative Council 
is currently reviewing the Air pollution Control Bill 2024, while the government has proposed 
maintai ning nitrogen dioxide targets (CAN, 2025). This Bill Committee is accepting public 
submissions now. Even a single letter is highly encouraged to paint the sky blue. 
 
Agnes is used to the air. That sentence should be unbearable. A city that has made peace 
with 3,508 preventable deaths a year has stopped being ambitious on behalf of its people. Hong 
Kong has the evidence, the policy architecture, and a neighbor that has already proved the 
alternative is possible. Now, it is time to make changes.
