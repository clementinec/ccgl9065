Fast fashion: The price we don ’t see 
 
Today’s fashion is cheaper, faster, and more convenient than ever. 
A few clicks, and a new outfit arrives at your door. But what we 
don’t see is the price paid elsewhere - in depleted rivers, 
poisoned fields, and the silent suffering of farming communities. 
For countries like Bangladesh, the rise of fast fashion has brought 
not prosperity, but a slow environmental collapse that directly 
threatens the land and the people who live from it. This essay 
addresses those hidden costs, showing the real impact of our 
disposable wardrobe on agriculture, and why the convenience we 
enjoy comes at a price most of us never pay. 
The current scale of worldwide textile production in staggering - 
with production nearly tripling from 2000 to today, now sitting at 
almost 150 billio n articles of clothing manufactured each year , 
which equates to around 12 to 16 articles of clothing for every 
person on earth. Rampant as it is - this trend shows no sign of 
stopping, with estimates suggesting textile manufacturing to only 
grow in the upcoming years. Consumers now -a-days are buying 
60% more clothing than they did 15 years ago and are keeping 
them for only half as long - leading to a culture of hyper -
disposab ility. To meet this demand, the industry has adopted a 
philosophy of ‘Dynamic Assortment ’ - which means coming up 
with new designs constantly - resulting in a significant portion of 
manufactured clothes not even being sold, instead getting 
dumped in landfills or incinerated . 
This enormous wave of manufacturing clothes comes at an 
equally enormous cost. Accounting for all the stages of 
production (raw material s to final product), garments consume a 

huge amount of water - from a simple cotton t -shirt that needs 
2,700 litres to a leather tote bag estimated to consume over 
17,000 litres of fresh water - around 8 0% of which is discharged 
back into nature as untreated wastewater carrying a toxic mixture 
of synthetic dyes, heavy metals, and other chemical s. Then there 
are the greenhouse gas emissions - fast fashion is now the 
second largest contributor to global CO 2 emmisions - accounting 
for about 10% , which is more than global aviation and maritime 
transport combined. Another less-known aspect of fast fashion ’s 
negative impact on the environment is it s emission of 
microplastics - synthetic fibers like polyester are the fourth 
largest source of primary microplastics in the oceans. Laundering 
these clothes releases roughly 500,000 tons of microfibers per 
year - the equivalent of dumping 50 billion plastic bottles into the 
sea. On top of all that, fast fashion also consumes colossal 
amounts of fossil fuels and other non -renewable resources - 98 
million tonnes of oil are estimated to be used each year to create 
synthetic fibers, and the textile industry also accounts for nearly 
20% of global insecticide use and 10% o f pesticide use. 
All these negative factors manifest as a death sen tence for the 
agriculture of nations like Bang ladesh. The waste water 
discharged as a by -product of textile manufacturing dam ages the 
rivers and canals - transforming them into toxic conduits of heavy 
metals and synthetic dyes. This industrial runoff also poisons the 
silt, chemically contaminating the soil which resu lts in long -term 
loss in cultiva tion viability. Furthermore, the industry's massive 
consumption of pesticides , fertilizers and insecticides creates a n 
"open loop" that collapses local biodiversity and exhausts the 
water table. For millions of farmers like my grandfather, this 
systemic extraction means that the very resources required for 

their food security and social mobility are being hijacked to fuel 
disposable global trends. A customer in the west buys clothes 
and accessories for cheap, and the price, in turn, is pai d by the 
farmers and their families with the ir food, livelihood and life. 
The numbers tell a simple truth: fast fashion is starving farmers in 
Bangladesh while filling closets in the West. But it’s still not too 
late. We can force brands to pay for recycling. We can incentivise 
farmers to grow clean cotton . We can ban the destruction of 
unsold clothes. These aren’t fantasies - they have precedent with 
laws already passed in Europe and Australia. What’s missing is a 
worldwide call to action . As consumers, we must demand 
durable, sustainable clothing and vote with our wallets 
accordingly . As global citizens, we must hold the fashion industry 
accountable for every litre of water stolen and every field 
poisoned. Change is possible - but only if we stop looking away .
