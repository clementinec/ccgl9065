The Water War: Man vs AI?

The Hidden Thirst of AI: Who Pays the Price?

In Mansfield, a small American town, Beverly Morris’s home is 366 metres away from a Meta AI data centre (AIDC). The AI cloud may be invisible, but her bucket is not: After construction began, her well pressure fell and sediment appeared, forcing her to carry water in buckets to flush her toilet (Fleury and Jimenez, 2025). While Meta disputes a connection, it remains undeniable that AIDCs drive water use. So, who bears the hidden water cost of AI?

The rise of AI may accelerate water inequality. AIDCs directly consumed an estimated 66 billion litres of water in 2023; By 2028, one single hyperscale centre could consume 60–124 billion litres of water annually, which approximately equals the annual water demand for 17 million people (Shehabi et al., 2024). Many AIDCs use evaporative cooling to cool facilities, evaporating around 80% of water used (Friends of the Earth, 2026; Li et al., 2025). Beyond cooling, electricity generation and chip manufacturing further intensify water demand (Shehabi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). Yet at the same time, around 1.8 billion people like Beverly still lack safe drinking water at their homes. Worse still, in 7 out of 10 such households, it is often women who are forced to carry heavy water buckets (WHO and UNICEF, 2023). This essay does not solely blame AI for all water shortage problems, but rather questions how individuals and companies can promote better water equality when stronger AI demands more water.

Technological advancement can make AI less thirsty. Starting in 2024, Microsoft has adopted a new cooling method that uses direct-to-chip and closed-loop cooling, reducing more than 125 million litres of cooling water annually (Solomon, 2024). This is promising, but it only replaces evaporative cooling, not the whole supply chain. Operators should use recycled or reclaimed water if appropriate, and avoid building water-intensive facilities in drought-prone areas.

Yet, technological advancements aren’t enough– We need your voice and actions.

First, audit your AI water footprint. We are not discouraging you from using AI, but we should use it responsibly: Try to avoid repeated questions and choose simpler tools for simple tasks. Every question you ask consumes about 10-50 ml of water (Li et al., 2025). Use tools like the Omni AI Water Footprint Calculator (https://l1nq.com/spaifuv) to estimate your water and share it with your friends. When we try to make the intangible tangible, awareness of AI-related environmental problems can be raised.

Secondly, instead of just comparing computing power, choose AI tools that bear environmental responsibility. Try to compare the Water Usage Efficiency (WUE) of different AI companies. You can also check Hugging Face’s public AI Energy Score leaderboard and choose the most efficient model that still does the job (Hugging Face, n.d.). One person alone cannot reform an industry, but informed customers can pressure tech giants to adopt the latest technology for better sustainability. In the drought-prone region of Chile, Cerrillos, residents voted against a proposed Google data centre construction in a local referendum, eventually pressuring Google to adopt a less water-intensive cooling system (McGovern and Branford, 2023). In Monterey Park, Los Angeles, residents protested against building a giant data centre in the region, worrying that AIDC will strain the energy grid and water supply. Community pressure has resulted in statutory reforms in California: AB 2619 now requires operators to report projected water use for permits and actual use when renewing licences, while AB 2469 requires drought planning for new or expanded centres (CalMatters, 2026a; Becker, 2026). This proves that market pressure can force AI tech giants to improve water and energy efficiency.

A full tap now does not guarantee an endless one. About 70-80% of Hong Kong(HK)’s fresh water comes from Dongjiang (Water Supplies Department, 2026). We may not face water shortage today, but our water security relies heavily on one shared river and careful planning. HK and China may follow the laws passed in California to require AI companies to disclose their water footprint and bear environmental responsibility. Yet, the whole transformation begins with a little more responsibility, a little more awareness and a little more pressure.

It is not a zero-sum game between water, people and AI development. Use AI responsibly, demand clear water data, and speak up for water-shortage safeguards now. The goal is not to stop the cloud, but to make sure it does not leave someone else’s tap dry. Act now, when the tap still flows. Rethink, to save your tokens, and to save the water.
