The Right to Have a Home

I want to start with a number that sounds like a mistake: twelve hundredmillion. By 2050, that is how many people may be displaced by climate change,and over two billion by the end of the century. In 2020, we already had morethan 80 million refugees (UNHCR, n.d.-a). I did not believe it at first. Then I realised I already knew some of these people.

They are the ones I have walked past for three years in Hong Kong. This cityholds one of the highest concentrations of millionaires in the world, and is alsoone of the most unequal places to live: about 1.4 million people live on lessthan HK$4,500 a month, a poverty rate of 21.4%, and roughly 13,000 asylumseekers and refugees survive alongside them in what has been called "theworld's least unaffordable real estate." I had never connected these people tothe climate crisis. I do now.

Because this crisis is not only about water. It is about law.

Here is what shocked me most. There is no such thing as a climate refugee.Under the 1951 Refugee Convention, you qualify only if you have awell-founded fear of persecution (UNHCR, n.d.-c). Rising seas do notpersecute anyone. So a family whose island is disappearing has no legalcategory to stand in. Entire island nations are now

certain to disappear withsea-level rise, and their people would arrive with no status at all (UNHCR,n.d.-a). As McDonnell (2018) reports, these are "the refugees the world barelypays attention to" - displaced by weather, not war, and therefore protectedby no one.

Think about what that actually means. A country with no war, no crime, andalmost no emissions can be sentenced to disappear - not for what it did, butfor what we did. Their people did the least to cause this, and they will be asked to leave first.

We spent the semester learning that climate change is a systems problem. Theweek on energy and transport showed me that our choices about power decidewho breathes clean air. The week on cities and buildings showed me that thebuilt environment decides who survives a heatwave. The week on capital andthe commons showed me that money flows towards whoever already haspower. By the time I reached the week on displacement, the pattern wasimpossible to miss: climate change does not create new injustice. It pours itselfinto the injustice that already exists.

That is why I am not proposing another technology. I am proposing a definition.

My solution is to build legal recognition for climate-displaced people - a protection status that does not require proof of persecution. It would beginwith regional

agreements: Pacific island states and their larger neighbourssigning binding commitments to accept people displaced by sea-level rise, withrights to work, housing and long-term support rather than temporary visas. Itwould mean governments formally recognising that sovereignty can continueeven when land does not. And it would give citizens of disappearing islandssomething they currently lack: a legal plan for the day the water arrives. Why this and not something else? Because the money alone is not arriving.The UN Environment Programme's Adaptation Gap Report 2025 finds thatdeveloping countries will need roughly $310 billion per year by 2035 forclimate adaptation, while international public adaptation finance stood at only$26 billion in 2023 - a gap of twelve to fourteen times (UNEP, 2025). A gapthat size is not solved by goodwill. It is solved by rules that decide who is owedprotection, and who pays for it. This matters in Hong Kong too, because a citythat already hosts 13,000 people in legal limbo has the institutions to helpdefine what comes next.

I know what the objection is. The numbers are contested, the scale isoverwhelming, and opening a new legal category could be slow, expensive andpolitically toxic. I take that seriously. But the alternative is what we have now:silence. A definition that does not exist protects no one. And the objection that'we cannot help everyone" has never been a reason to help no one.

So this is what I would ask you to do. When climate displacement appears inyour news feed, do not scroll past the word "refugee" as if it were settled. Askinstead: under what law? If your answer is "none," then you have found thegap I am talking about. Push the institution you belong to -a university, acompany, a city - to formally recognise climate displacement. Support thecampaigns, and vote for the people who will write the definition.

Because the sea is rising whether or not we have a word for it. And a personwho loses a country should not also lose the right to be recognised.
