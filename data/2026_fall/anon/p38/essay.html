Invisible Data but Physical Storage Cost

Digital Sufficiency Alongside Corporate Accountability for Climate Resistance

Since the 21st century, the rapid growing internet has brought uncountable

convenience to the world, becoming an inseparable part of modern society. However, the

heavy cost behind this progress has gone unnoticed by the general public, being

overshadowed by the immense advantages. Our digital data, instead of being weightless and

invisible, is all stored in the physical network of data centres, at the price of staggering

amounts of electricity and water, contributing to the greenhouse effect at every moment. To

resolve this ongoing crisis, we must embrace digital sufficiency, a custom every citizen could

easily achieve through periodic checks on one's digital storage. Governments must also work

with companies, internalizing the true environmental costs so that sustainability is truly

considered and fostered.

The key mechanism behind the greenhouse gas emissions is a class of chemicals

called hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), which are used to cool cloud servers and prevent

overheating, acting as the backbone of the data centres' cooling system. Ironically, however,

once these gases trap heat and leak into the atmosphere, they act as notorious greenhouse

gases with a global warming potential up to 14,700 times that of carbon dioxide under the

IPCC's latest assessment, making it a huge contributor to the climate issue (Environmental

Investigation Agency, 2024). Besides this, resources used to maintain data centres were

massive. According to the International Energy Agency (2025), global data centres, in 2024,

consumed not only approximately 415 terawatt-hours of electricity, but also nearly 560

billion litres of water for the cooling process. The above statistics clearly showcase the

drastic amount of resources demanded to operate the whole physical cooling system, so as to

keep the virtual world functioning.

One of the existing "solutions" often used by tech companies as a response to such a

phenomenon was buying carbon offsets, financially funding green projects to compensate for

their emissions. But this was rather superficial, acting as a symptomatic treatment that

allowed companies to bypass tackling the root of the issue. As noted by Naoto in his audit

(2026), carbon offsets "become an alibi when they displace responsibility from the actual site

of environmental burden to an accounting mechanism," enabling corporations to claim

carbon neutrality while neglecting the continuous expansion in resource depletion. It neither

curbed the physical demand nor addressed the usage of HFC refrigerants; it only served as an

excuse that allowed the whole society to procrastinate on the unchanged greenhouse issue.

Genuine progress began only when we transferred our attention to measures that

directly affected energy consumption. As for individuals, the most straightforward yet

unnoticeable solution would be to adopt the mindset of digital sufficiency, to proactively

reduce digital reliance and virtual data. Some good practices were simply deleting duplicate

photos, discarding old emails, or any other events relevant to lowering storage. It might seem

insignificant, but the aggregate effect of millions of data removed would trigger a long-term

positive cascade that directly lessened the baseline demand for resources. As summarized by

Stanford Magazine, every 100 gigabytes of data would produce an annual 400 - 500 pounds

of carbon emissions, which was equivalent to powering an average home for 2 - 3 weeks

(Stanford Magazine, 2017, as cited in Varcho, 2026). Once everyone started periodically

cleaning redundant files or removing unused data, these seemingly minor habits accumulated

into a substantial drop in global electricity demand and carbon output, saving tons of energy

to support unavoidable powering.

However, individual efforts alone were insufficient under corporations' reckless

expansions to power the internet. Tech giants were rarely held accountable for the

environmental damages because the environmental impacts were often treated as external

costs that were imposed on the public and the planet rather than the companies themselves.

Without restrictions, companies would remain prioritizing their profits by expanding cloud

storage while ignoring environment fallouts, which became tragedy of commons. Addressing

such an issue required holding the firms accountable by making them internalize the cost of

their footprints. This was already in progress, with several U.S. states, including New Jersey

and Pennsylvania, done enforcing legislation that required formal submissions of reports for

data centres on their energy and water usage (Data Center Energy and Water Reporting Act,

2026). Other countries like Finland have also proposed similar legislation that demands

electricity consumption and water use (Dimou, 2026). We should promote these reporting to

become universal, facilitating transparency so that further actions could be precisely

calibrated, such as binding strict resource pricing and extraction caps. Only when companies

are forced to pay the true cost can they break away from the ongoing limitless expansion and

shift the entire focus back toward genuine sustainability.

Ultimately, everyone should be mindful of the obscured costs behind the virtual world

that not only causes a heavy resource burden but also contributes to the greenhouse effect. A

solution to ameliorate this phenomenon will be adopting digital sufficiency and combining it

with systemic corporate accountability, which will cease infinite extraction of resources and

draw back attention at sustainability.
