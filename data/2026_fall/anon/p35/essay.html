Protecting Hong Kong’s Corals Before the Boat Leaves

When I went diving a few months ago in Sai Kung, I paid little attention to the corals. Hong Kong’s 2024 coral bleaching event reminds me of that diving experience. Anchoring near coral communities would cause more damage to those already suffering from heat stress (Flynn & Forrester, 2019). It makes me wonder if a shared map before departure could help boat operators to choose the stop point to avoid adding to the stress on recovering corals.

Hong Kong’s 2024 Coral Bleaching Event

Coral bleaching is a process in which coral turns white due to changes in conditions like temperature, light, or nutrients (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration [NOAA], 2026). Heat stress can cause corals to expel symbiotic algae that live in corals’ tissues. The bleaching could be improved if the stress is relieved. Ocean warming is making marine heatwaves more frequent and intense on a global scale, which brings a higher risk of mass coral bleaching (Cooley et al., 2022). Hong Kong Reef Check (HKRC)’s 2024 survey found that coral bleaching occurred in 13 of 33 tested sites (Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation Department [AFCD], 2024). A joint survey linked the July 2024 coral bleaching event to sustained high seawater temperatures (AFCD, 2025). Follow-up surveys found that corals at most affected sites were gradually recovering (AFCD, 2024). What could we do to protect corals during the recovery period?

Course Perspectives on Local Marine Conservation

The Week 8 case study shows that Hong Kong’s trawling ban brought about signs of recovery in some marine communities, though the results varied across different sites. It provides a perspective for thinking about this question: reducing one type of human stress can lead to improvement. During the recovery period, one of the main external stresses is damage caused by the ship’s anchor. Reducing anchoring in the recovering coral would help. The Week 10 discussion of cause-and-effect chains helped me understand that when surveyors discover bleached coral, they should update the information to boat operators. The operators could then adjust their anchoring position to reduce the possibility of anchoring near the injured coral community.

Proposed Pre-Departure Anchoring Advisory

A small pilot program could be launched in Sai Kung for dive boats heading to Sharp Island. When surveyors confirm coral bleaching near the island, the AFCD could work with local boat and dive operators by labelling the areas where bleaching has been confirmed on a shared map. It should include affected areas, current no-anchoring zones and confirmed alternative mooring points. AFCD could date each version and publish it in operators’ group chat, with a QR code linking to the latest version of the map. Boat operators should check the updated map before departure. If all suitable mooring spots are unavailable, operators should change dive sites or reschedule before departure. Sharing the map at this stage could give operators time to change their plan before leaving the dock.

Pilot Evaluation

However, if several operators choose the same alternative dive site, this guide could shift the anchoring pressure from Sharp Island to a nearby place. In this case, the pilot needs to track where the boat will anchor after changing plans. According to a survey in the British Virgin Islands, more boats choose sites with mooring buoys, which caused the density of boats anchored on coral reefs to become roughly half compared with sites without moorings (Forrester, 2020). Therefore, in Sai Kung’s pilot, AFCD should record the number of both total visiting boats and those anchored near corals at Sharp Island and nearby alternative sites, respectively. This would help in judging whether the advisory reduces anchoring around stressed corals or transfers the pressure to another bay.

Before booking my next dive in Sai Kung, I would ask where the boat plans to stop and whether it needs to anchor. A map shared before departure could make it easier for boat operators to choose a suitable stop point that would not damage the recovering corals.

Conclusion

In conclusion, anchoring would add more stress on corals that already suffer from heat stress. The Sai Kung pilot uses the coral monitoring results in planning the dive trips. It allows boat operators to know where they should avoid anchoring before departure and select a suitable alternative stop point. It could reduce avoidable damage to corals around Sharp Island during the bleaching recovery period.
