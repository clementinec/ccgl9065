A Greener City

Beyond Technology: Building a Just and Sustainable City Imagine that I am a policymaker who has the responsibility of transforming the city into a sustainable, low-carbon society. Given the fact that our resources are limited, where should I start? Should I introduce a carbon tax immediately? Should I invest in renewable energy? Or should I reward those who protect the environment? Before taking this course, I believed that such climate issues could be solved through technological advancement. However, after a series of debates and discussion, I noticed that these problems may affect multiple sectors and social groups. Thus, I would need to draw the bigger picture of the issues and try to maximize the overall benefit. As a policymaker, one thing I want to invest in the most is renewable energy. According to the Institute for Energy Research (2026), fossil fuels still account for approximately 86%. Although renewable energy now is constantly growing, fossil fuels are still in the dominant position. Therefore, transforming the city into a renewable energy-based city may seem to be a simple answer to climate change. However, there is a major problem with this transformation, the cost. Let’s take wind power as an example, in order to develop such technology, we would need materials whose prices may rise due to increasing demand. Such price spikes could delay the deployment by 13% and increase the installation cost by $228 billion (Yuan et al., 2025). Thus, I realized that technology development can be limited by economic problems and here comes to our second problem: where should our funding come from? Funding is always a challenge for any public policy. The first thing that comes to my mind is a carbon tax. Introducing a carbon tax and establishing an emissions trading system can generate government revenue. According to the World Bank (2026), carbon pricing generated $107 billion in 2025. Thus, the government can use some of the revenue to invest in renewable energy and energy transition. Funding can also come from private corporations. In order to attract enterprises to invest, the government can ensure a stable regulatory environment and provide loan guarantees. By reducing uncertainty and market risks, private investors may be more willing to invest in the industry (International Energy Agency [IEA], 2025). However, introducing a carbon tax can lead companies to pass the costs on to customers, which

may increase the cost of living for the public (Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development [OECD] & United Nations Development Programme [UNDP], 2025). I realised that another issue I need to address is the system itself. Although the introduction of carbon pricing is to make those who pollute take responsibility, the burden can eventually be borne by the public due to the interconnected economic system. Merkle and Dolphin (2024) developed a mathematical model to examine how increased costs due to carbon tax could be passed on to customers. They found that the effect of carbon pricing towards household expenditure has a regressive impact in all 19 EU countries examined in 2020, which means households with lower income may face a greater relative financial burden. This is obviously not the purpose of such policy. I was surprised that a good intention can result in a totally different outcome. The policy designed to protect our environment may end up adding difficulties to those who are already financially vulnerable. As a policymaker, I started to ask myself a question: What is the point of creating a green city where people there can’t even afford their basic needs? Enforcing a carbon tax can indeed increase government revenue and boost energy transition but the consequences may include unfair distribution of financial burdens. Therefore, I propose a three-part policy. First, we introduce a carbon tax gradually, instead of putting a huge burden on companies and customers all of a sudden, which allows them to prepare and adapt to it. Second, we can provide subsidies to households with relatively lower income and those who suffer from climate change to ensure fair allocation of such revenue. Finally, we can invest the remaining funds in research and deployment of renewable energy. The implementation of these measures and the allocation of funds should remain flexible. For example, if a severe climate disaster occurs, we can allocate more funds to help those affected by the incident as supplementary financial support. By launching this plan, we can transform the city into an eco-friendly place and do our best to minimize the unequal impacts. To sum up, I was not someone who cared much about climate change, as I believed that technology could solve most of the problems. However, after a semester of learning, I realized that technology alone cannot solve climate change. From the perspective of a policymaker, I now understand that building a sustainable city
