Who Gets to Sleep? Cooler Homes for Hong Kong’s Hotter Nights

One hour of air-conditioning for a chance of eight hours of sound sleep. In a

windowless apartment in Hong Kong. Simon and Elaine Ho cooled the room before bed, just

to wake up a few hours later when the heat returned (Delmotte, 2023). If you cannot even

ensure sleep in your own apartment, can you really call it home? As Hong Kong’s nights get

hotter, we should urge housing authorities to check bedroom temperatures at night and how

heat transfers whenever an older building is due for a major renovation.

According to the Hong Kong Observatory, from 1995 to 2014, Hong Kong averaged

around 21 hot nights per year, when temperatures never went below 28°C (Hong Kong

Observatory, n.d.). As greenhouse gas emissions warm the planet, the number of hot nights

will only increase. According to HKO, under intermediate emission scenarios, the number of

hot nights will increase to a drastic 117 per year in 2081 to 2100 (Hong Kong Observatory,

n.d.)! For cities facing hotter nights, the IPCC pinpoints how heat flows into and out of

buildings as a starting point for change (Dodman et al., 2022, p. 924). Thus, Hong Kong

should consider the effects of housing conditions such as the thermal resistance, presence of

solar shading and ventilation of apartments on nighttime room temperature during

maintenance checks in older housing buildings.

The heat that woke up the Hos may have built up during the day. Housing materials

such as concrete, asphalt, and glass absorb heat and release it after sunset, contributing to the

urban heat island effect. A study on Hong Kong housing writes that “The synergetic effect of

UHI and heatwaves can further amplify urban overheating and increase building cooling

load” (Feng et al., 2026, Section 1, para. 3). The same study found that top floors generally

require more cooling than bottom floors in their simulations and identifies “the greater heat

gain through the exposed roof” (Feng et al., 2026, Section 4.1, para. 3) as the reason behind

this phenomenon. Following the lecture on democracy and capitalism’s shift from buildings

to decisions, we ask who decides on which aspect of the problem? Since occupants control

the air-conditioning, while the housing authority controls the roof. Residents should urge

authorities to inspect the roof before the next repair. If results confirm the roof as a major heat

source, test a reflective coating. Another separate experiment concludes that a reflective roof

averages a ceiling temperature 2°C lower than a conventional roof on sunny days(Zhao et al.,

2026). For other parts of the flat, match the repair to how heat enters and gets trapped. If

sunlight through the windows is the problem, ask for external shades. Likewise, if the room

stays hot even after outside air cooling, ask for an improved ventilation system (Dodman et

al., 2022, Section 6.3.5.2).

Some may argue that limited housing funds should prioritise urgent repairs instead,

and this deserves acknowledgement. Hence, without delaying essential repairs, we propose to

first test cooling improvements during scheduled maintenance, then weigh the extra cost

against potential electricity savings and better sleep quality, before wider implementation.

Moreover, the wider climate benefit of weakening the positive feedback loop that makes

air-conditioning more necessary should also be considered. In 2023, air-conditioning was

responsible for roughly 40% of Hong Kong’s residential electricity (Electrical and

Mechanical Services Department, 2025, p. 37). To generate the electricity needed, more fossil

fuels will be burned, releasing more carbon emissions and worsening warming. This

increases demand for air-conditioning, and the cycle repeats. This is a classic positive

feedback loop, a form of cascade effect, and our solution directly undermines it from the

source, weakening the cycle. Furthermore, if less air-conditioning is required after sunset, it

could ease the rise in electricity demand at night, flattening the steeper part of the Duck

Curve and making solar energy a more viable renewable energy source in Hong Kong. This

further weakens the feedback loop. Some may say one roof will barely affect climate change,

but if we stack the electricity savings across millions of flats in a city that heavily relies on

air-conditioning, we can cut the carbon emissions that make the world hotter for everyone.

That wider change can begin with the heat in your room. If your room stays hot after

sunset, ask your neighbours for opinions. Together, ask your estate office to investigate and

make nighttime heat part of their next repair plan. The next roof repair could mean a cooler

room and a lower electricity bill. The Hos’ story of one hour of air-conditioning for a mere

chance of eight hours of sleep should give Hong Kong more than enough of a reason to act. Is

asking for a home that lets you sleep at night really too much to ask for?
