From Cotton Fields to Landfills

Cheap Clothes, Expensive Wreckage: How Fast Fashion Exploits the Planet

Buying a 50 HKD item on Shein or Taobao feels like a steal, and the wardrobe is quickly piled with cheap clothes of poor quality, all for less than $100. The next day, those clothes are thrown away after one wear because they are either not for daily occasions or lose shape after just one wash. Somewhere far away, where cotton is grown, the environment is destroyed without us being aware.

The processes of producing textiles are famously costing us too much. Cotton, for instance, is the most common and accounts for over 80% of natural fiber use (Şener Fidan et al., 2023) and created a fashion empire with its softness. According to Chapagain et al. (2006), one T-shirt is estimated to need 2700 liters of water, and the UN (2022) reported that the fashion industry generates 2- 8% of global greenhouse gas emissions while using millions of Olympic-sized swimming pools' worth of water every year. Not only cotton, all natural fiber production also requires a surprisingly huge amount of water, takes up land for animals and trees, and causes soil degradation, not to mention the pesticides and fertilization used. And although overconsumption of clothes is in the global north, it is the producing countries, mostly the global south, that bear the consequences of degrading land and factory emissions. Synthetic fiber, on the other hand, is nothing better: produced from fossil fuels, the

carbon footprint of a ton of polyester is approximately 21,000 kgCO2e in the UK (Thomas et al., 2012), and they release tens of thousands of microfibers with every wash, resulting in an estimated 51 trillion microplastic particles in the ocean (UN, 2024). Even worse, the “crazy” fashion industry is not only linked to environmental destruction but is also well-known for human rights issues.

Fast fashion giant Shein has always been known for cheap clothing and rapid growth but is always mentioned when fast fashion emission problems and inhumane treatment issues emerge. Shein reported a CO2 emission in 2023 that was almost double that in 2022, and this is partly caused by the enormous scale of the business: at any time, 600,000 items are on sale. The overproduction is not an accident, but a systematic issue tightly linked with the natural desire of humans and the economic incentive of generating profit. Shein was reported to have profits of 2 billion (BBC News, 2024) while producing 1,228,000 metric tons of waste in 2026 (Winther, 2026). Yet, when facing criticisms, the company often refuses to answer or distracts the public (Mulkey, 2024).

Hong Kong is deeply entangled in this global system as we import massive quantities of fast fashion. Gen Z-favorite brands like Brandy Melville, Cotton On, H&M, Zara, and all the e-commerce platforms are part of the fast fashion overconsumption system. Around 400 tons of clothes are discarded daily in Hong

Kong (Redress, 2025), and this number piles up to more than 110,000 tons per year, which is 1,400 T-shirts per minute and could cover 25,000 Hong Kong Stadiums, according to Greenpeace (2015). Meanwhile, the same synthetic fabrics we wear shed microplastics into our coastal waters every time we wash them. We are all in the system of creating climate damage for everyone to pay.

Although promotion against overconsumption solves some of the issue, it is inappropriate to blame only the individuals; systematic accountability is also required. The Hong Kong government can impost a mandatory extended producer responsibility scheme on fashion retailers, which makes brands pay for the production costs and fund textile sorting and recycling infrastructure. Critics may argue that this will raise clothing prices for low-income families. However, the current pricing is only an illusion risking the global environment. We are already paying for fast fashion through the public cost of waste management and the health impacts of microplastics. Clothing manufacturing factories and brands are producing easily broken clothes faster than consumers could buy, and doesn’t throwing clothes away after only one wear create even more costs than buying something that lasts long? By making brands absorb the true cost, we force them to design durable, circular clothing.

In conclusion, the bargain of a $50 shirt is not what it seems, the lowered price is paid by both exploited workers and the environment, with the cost bared by the global

south. Although a scheme may ask brands to take some of the responsibilities, we as individuals should also review our consuming habits and make efforts in changing the way the world shops.
