Cooling for Climate Justice

A summer night in a Hong Kong subdivided flat can be hotter indoors than outdoors. In a survey of 31 Kwai Chung households, apparent indoor temperature reached 44°C after dark at relative humidity above 72 per cent; over 70 per cent reported heat-related symptoms, and 65 per cent avoided air-conditioning because of electricity costs (Ho, 2025). Indoor heat is thus a housing condition, not a meteorological event.

Approximately 220,000 people occupy 110,000 subdivided units (Ho, 2025), and the Observatory recorded 61 hot nights in 2021, the most since 1884 (Lai et al., 2026). Risk is spatially concentrated: building-level heat-health risk in Sham Shui Po ranges from 0.152 to 0.845 at night, peaking where overcrowding exceeds 400 per cent (Lai et al., 2026). The stakes extend beyond housing. Suicide claims an estimated 720,000 lives yearly, and temperature-attributable suicide is projected to rise most sharply in warmer regions, including Southeast Asia, partly through sleep disruption (Ro et al., 2026). This essay argues that Hong Kong should apply solar-reflective coatings to all subdivided-unit buildings by 2030, prioritising the highest-risk blocks, and finance the work through utility energy-efficiency funds rather than tenant charges.

Coatings modify building fabric rather than occupant behaviour, which matters where residents work long shifts and cannot alter ventilation or energy use. Alternatives are weaker. Rebuilding serves future rather than current occupants, and greening offers limited relief in dense tropical developments: an additional green park reduced Singaporean urban heat island intensity by 0.022°C, and green plot ratio correlated negatively with cooling (−0.45) (Aydin et al., 2025). Coatings instead raise solar reflectance, so less radiation is absorbed by the façade and re-emitted indoors (Wong, 2025).

Evidence exists at scale. A pilot across 88 Tampines blocks reduced ambient temperature by up to 2°C; it will extend to all HDB estates by 2030 at an additional S$60 million, with government funding the premium over conventional paint, and residents consumed less electricity as air-conditioning demand fell (Wong, 2025). Hong Kong has trialled passive radiative cooling paint and applied it to the Coliseum roof (Legislative Council, 2025); the constraint is coverage, not feasibility.

Coverage should be allocated by building rather than district, since the risk index identifies blocks exceeding the 400 per cent threshold (Lai et al., 2026), and delivered through existing mechanisms: CLP’s 50 million for 70,000 low-income households and HEC’s 1.2 million for 1,200 subdivided-unit households (Legislative Council, 2025). Support should be targeted, as over 70 per cent of EU measures were untargeted and the £150 Warm Home Discount proved “a drop in the ocean” (Croon & Lausberg, 2023, p. 4). One objection requires attention. Green–blue adaptation across 5,503 African municipalities raised housing prices 13.0 per cent and population 52.9 per cent; rent control alone intensified displacement pressure (0.377 against 0.208), whereas resettlement support attenuated it (0.311 against 0.764) (Kim et al., 2026). Coatings should therefore be conditional on tenancy protection and metering regulation, since landlords rather than utilities set subdivided-flat electricity prices, and only 153 prosecutions followed 2,941 overcharging reports (Delina et al., 2026).

Benefits would concentrate among those least able to avoid heat. Of 1,393 older adults in Yau Tsim Mong and Kwun Tong, 60 per cent screened frail; frailty was associated with more heat symptoms (RR 1.28), and living alone protected robust but not frail participants, whose reduced capacity to leave a hot dwelling compounds exposure (Lai et al., 2025). Cooling centres are inadequate: 19 shelters over 70 days averaged nine registered users nightly, which the Government treats as sufficient (Legislative Council, 2025), while 85.7 per cent of industrial-building subdivided-flat residents reported heatstroke symptoms (CarbonCare InnoLab & Kwai Chung Subdivided Flat Community Association, 2024).

Two actions remain available. Residents may locate their building on the district heat-risk map and request coating from the relevant District Council; they may also inform older tenants of the CLP Inverter Air Conditioner Replacement Subsidy, $5 million for approximately 1,200 households, noting that only 36.3 per cent know such support exists (Delina et al., 2026; Legislative Council, 2025).

Indoor heat in subdivided housing is a product of building fabric, not climate alone. Reflective coatings convert an unaffordable cooling problem into a capital works programme; until applied at scale, 220,000 residents will inhabit temperatures the rest of the city does not endure.
