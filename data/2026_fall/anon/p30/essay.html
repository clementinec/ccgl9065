Who Pays for Hong Kong’s Climate Goals?

Consider the owner of a cha chan teng in Sham Shui Po, who has just opened her electricity bill and is wondering if a new carbon price will impact her shop in the coming month due to the many appliances she uses. Her question is essentially about the cost associated with electricity use, given that electricity generation contributed roughly 61% of Hong Kong's carbon emissions in 2024 (Hong Kong SAR Government, n.d.) – but it really speaks to the fundamental question: who pays for Hong Kong's climate objectives? Hong Kong intends to achieve carbon neutrality before 2050, while achieving a 50% emissions reduction from 2005 levels by 2035 (Hong Kong SAR Government, n.d.). There will be some kind of cost distribution in Hong Kong, among government, businesses and households. Similarly, through much of this course, the same tension was revealed, whether it was for affordable fashion or for ocean conservation and livelihoods. At the end of these cases, the strongest arguments defended both the people (by helping to cover some costs) and climate action goals. Unlike other instruments of change, a carbon tax confronts this issue head-on. I believe that a policy of a carbon pricing scheme on fossil fuel-based emissions would work best if a carbon tax was coupled with a reasonable, fair dividend of that cost back to individuals as well. Evidence that pricing has an effect is strong; Andersson (2019) found that both the carbon tax and tax on transport fuel in Sweden decreased transport emissions by nearly 11% relative to a simulated control group, suggesting that pricing works most effectively with viable substitution alternatives to the taxed activity. Murray and Rivers (2015) also studied the impact of British Columbia's tax and found emissions cut approximately between 5 and 15%, with almost no measurable effect on overall economic activity. Design is just as important, if not more important, than headline rates. British Columbia made its carbon tax revenue neutral; gave each resident a Climate Action Credit in the form of a 100(30 for children) annual payment (Province of British Columbia, 2008). It was also noted that trust in a policy is difficult to build; the federal fuel charge in Canada had a monthly rebate system but still was removed on 1 April 2025 (Government of Canada, 2025). Carattini et al. (2018) identified fears about the costs, fairness, and the use of revenue as barriers to adoption of climate policy and therefore I believe that transparency should be a part of the policy design process. In Hong Kong, I envision the policy of the lowest possible price on carbon, increasing it at a measured pace over many years, and targeting the lowest income households through an efficient government channel, along with grants to small stores who purchase qualifying energy efficient equipment.

The immediate advantage would go to people living on the lowest incomes and small businesses, as the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (2023) suggests that the money from carbon pricing could be used to support poor households. Everyone benefits from a policy that results in lower emissions and is easy to trust. You can start taking action now without the need to wait for a proposal for a new carbon policy; whenever someone puts forward a carbon policy, consider asking three questions: who will pay? Who will be protected? Who will evaluate the outcome? Furthermore, you can get in touch with your Legislative Council member asking that the carbon pricing proposal contain a plan detailing how the generated money will be used. In the instance of the café owner, that would mean an opportunity to receive a dividend to reduce her expenditure and possibly receive an investment grant towards the purchase of a power-saving fridge. People can only feel inspired and motivated if the carbon price appears to relate directly to them. If the café owner knows that the proceeds will return to her and that she can have access to some capital investment for equipment improvements, then her engagement in climate action will transition from being viewed as something punitive towards being considered an action that she may want to take part in.
