Our Trends, Their Trash

-Tracing the journey of Trashion: Our Trends, Their TrashLast winter, I cleared out the old clothes in my wardrobe without hesitation, assuming they do not matter to me anymore. But what I did not acknowledge is that they just vanished from a Hong Kong closet only to reappear in a landfill—never really disappearing in this world. In Ghana, around 15 million second-hand garments (Gelbart, 2025) from all around the globe arrive every week. These garments find a second life by being resold and remade at the Kantamanto Market. It sounds like a fairytale, but not all garments share the same future. Damaged garments and torn fibres often end up as waste sitting on beaches or rotting on the bottom of lagoons, leaving local communities bothered. Decluttering frees the wardrobe–yet it fills up distant landfills. Clothes could be buried, but their true cost can never be hidden. Apparently, responsibility is the only thing not included in the receipt. The consequence is clear: cheap clothing does not eliminate costs, it just induces new ones and imposes them on the environment and garment workers. In Hong Kong, an average of 402 tonnes of textiles (Redress, 2025) entered landfills daily in 2023. Natural fibres in landfills release methane as they decompose, trapping heat in the atmosphere. However, the environmental cost does not end when clothes enter a landfill. Clothing wastes also contribute to marine pollution, killing off sea animals. According to HK01, a beaked whale died stranded at Tai O, with canvas bags in its digestive system. The incident reflects a wider problem: our culture of fast fashion and impulsive consumption leaves an everlasting burden on innocent marine life. It is an instant gratification for us, but a lasting cost for the ecosystem. Learning how our impulsive shopping harms the environment calls for immediate action. In terms of legislation, the Hong Kong Government could introduce extended producer responsibility for textile wastes. In 2007, France made an effort by introducing a textile responsibility system for recycling footwear and textiles. (ADEME, n.d.) Through Refashion, various clothing companies are motivated by financial incentives to help collect, sort, repair and recycle unwanted clothes. Brands are also rewarded for extending the lives of clothes, encouraging them to support upcycling. Specifically, Hong Kong could learn from France’s “eco-modulation” fee model, helping to collect funds for subsidizing sorting facilities. This in turn promotes sustainable fashion. As for producers and consumers, they could build a more circular fashion. According to the WTO, circular fashion helps change the habit of buying and discarding by introducing alternatives. Brands contribute by making clothes that last longer and are easy to repair, while consumers could make an effort by swapping clothes or buying second hand ones. When old garments are no longer wearable, the materials could be recycled and made into new clothes, giving them a second life. Ultimately, the amount of textiles being sent to the landfill decreases and reduces the need for new materials. The aim is not to discard the clothes elsewhere, but to stop their toxic cycle of becoming waste. These actions could promote sustainable fashion across Hong Kong. Second hand clothes, swapping and repairing give citizens an alternative to buying new ones, encouraging them to cherish what they have. They can begin with one practical step: starting a one month no new clothes trend, or trading clothes at local stores like Green Ladies. With adequate promotion in learning about consequences brought by fast fashion, citizens would be motivated to change their habits in hopes of safeguarding

the future of the oceans and the environment. It allows them to gain more control over their wardrobe choices, making fashion more ethical and sustainable. Sustainable fashion promotes a stronger implication: It changes our values towards what we consider as a fair price for clothing. Clothes are not truly cheap if someone has to deal with the wastes and aftermath. Brands should not be indifferent once sales have been made and damage has been caused. Upcycling clothes makes us reflect on our attitude towards "Conspicuous consumption”. It encourages us to value what we already own, rather than constantly looking for replacements to fulfill our gratification. The excitement of a new outfit should not come at the expense of our future far from the checkout counter. Our goal is not simply to keep clothes out of landfills, but stop inflicting the consequences that were made for fulfilling our desires onto innocent people and animals. Let “Our Trends, Their Trash” be a slogan in the past–let’s change what we buy and what we keep to secure a bright future for all.
