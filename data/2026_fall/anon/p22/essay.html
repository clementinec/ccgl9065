We Are Air-Conditioning Ourselves into a Hotter Future

Break The Heat Cycle: Rethinking Hong Kong’s Dependence on Air-Conditioning

On a hot summer afternoon in Hong Kong, stepping into an air-conditioned shopping mall or office feels like immediate relief. However, this comfort carries a cost beyond electricity bills. Air-conditioners function by transferring heat from indoor spaces to the outdoors, releasing waste heat into an already warm urban environment. As temperatures rise, Hong Kong’s residents become increasingly dependent on cooling systems. But, the more the city relies on airconditioning, the more heat is released into the surrounding environment. This creates a vicious self-reinforcing cycle: the temporary solution to rising temperatures makes the underlying problem worse.

This is not just a local issue: Climate change has been increasing temperatures and making extreme heat more frequent. Hong Kong is particularly vulnerable to these effects because of its dense buildings, extensive concrete surfaces and limited green space. According to the Electrical and Mechanical Services Department1, air-conditioning accounted for approximately 30% of Hong Kong’s total electricity consumption in 2023. Meanwhile, the Hong Kong Observatory2 recorded 54 hot nights in 2025, which was the third highest annual total since records began in 1884. These figures show how heavily the city depends on cooling systems while facing increasingly persistent heat.

Recent research conducted in Sai Ying Pun found that temperatures in urban street-canyons could rise by 1-3oC above the surrounding ambient temperatures during summertime, with air-conditioning exhaust and other human activities contributing to this warming (Huang et al., 20203). As outdoor temperatures increase, residents and businesses would require more cooling, potentially intensifying the urban heatisland effect. Therefore, this issue is not simply about individual electricity consumption, it is also about how Hong Kong designs and manages its urban environment.

Hong Kong needs a practical response addressing both cooling demand and outdoor heat. The government should adopt a two-pronged strategy focusing on building efficiency and public infrastructure. Together, these measures can reduce dependence on cooling systems while making the city more resilient to extreme heat.

The first prong would focus on improving buildings: The government should introduce stronger passive-cooling requirements for new developments and major renovations. These standards should encourage the construction of green roofs, improved insulation, external shading, and natural ventilation. Existing buildings should also receive financial incentives to retrofit outdated cooling systems with energy-efficient equipment. Simply asking residents to use less airconditioning would not address the buildings that trap heat in the first place, so, by designing buildings that can remain cooler naturally, Hong Kong can reduce electricity demand and the waste heat ejected by cooling systems. Buildings should be designed to prevent trapping excessive heat rather than relying entirely on machines to remove it.

The second prong should focus on transforming public spaces. The government should establish a citywide urban cooling programme prioritising tree planting, shaded walkways and green infrastructure. Trees can provide shade and cool their surroundings through evapotranspiration, while green roofs and walls can reduce heat absorption. Public spaces should also incorporate shaded areas, allowing residents to not have to constantly seek refuge in air-conditioned spaces. This is particularly important for outdoor workers, elderly residents and people who cannot afford constant cooling. This prong is important as research on Hong Kong’s urban heat island found that residential cooling energy demand in urban planning areas could be up to 5 times higher than in rural areas (Liu et al., 20254), demonstrating why improving the urban environment is essential.

These reforms would be able to benefit both individuals and the wider community. Better building design could lower electricity bills and reduce dependence on cooling systems, while greener public spaces would improve residents’ everyday experience. More importantly, these changes would be able to address the unequal burden of extreme heat: People who work outdoors or live in poorly ventilated homes often have fewer opportunities to escape high temperatures. A cooler urban environment could then provide protection beyond those who can afford private cooling systems.
