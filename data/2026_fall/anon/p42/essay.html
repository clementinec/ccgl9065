Ocean Needs Both

On a hot 33-degree afternoon, a grandmother in a crowded Kwun Tong estate looks for the closest shaded park designated for redevelopment. The only cool outdoor spot she can find is the corridor of her building. Her family has been waiting years for a bigger apartment, but each new housing project seems to mean losing a small park. By the end of 2024, public rental applicants had been waiting an average of 5.3 years.(Hong Kong Housing Authority and Housing Department [HKHA], 2024). Her dilemma is the city-wide trade-off where housing and green space are treated as rivals.

This issue isn't just limited to Hong Kong. Around the world, cities are experiencing more intense heat waves, and the people most affected tend to be those who are economically and socially on the margins, living in crowded neighborhoods.(Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change [IPCC], 2022). In Hong Kong, the dense development, crowded public spaces, and lack of greenery make the city noticeably warmer than the countryside, increasing heat stress and energy consumption (Legislative Council Secretariat [LegCo], 2021). This issue hits home for many families who need stable, comfortable homes. Without enough shade, plants, and good ventilation, new housing estates risk repeating the same overheated and stressful living conditions that housing policies aim to improve.

The solution is to stop treating housing and green infrastructure as mutually exclusive and instead embed vertical and pocket green space inside every new residential development. Rooftop gardens, vertical plant walls, podium planting strips, ground-floor pocket parks and permeable paving can be designed into blocks from the outset, rather than added as decoration after the units are built.

This approach works because vegetation cools the built environment directly. Local measurements show that green roofs can reduce Hong Kong roof surface temperatures by roughly 18 to 26 degrees Celsius in August, while providing insulation that lowers cooling demand on upper floors (Architectural Services Department [ArchSD], 2007). More broadly, shaded vegetated surfaces can be 11 to 25 degrees Celsius cooler than unshaded ones, and trees cool pedestrian-level air more effectively than grass alone (LegCo, 2021). IPCC evidence likewise identifies urban greening and other nature-based solutions as mainstream, effective adaptation options that reduce urban heat and heat stress while improving resident well-being (IPCC, 2022).

Hong Kong should improve on its current rules instead of creating a new system. Right now, guidelines require at least 20% greenery for sites over 1,000 square meters and 30% for larger sites, with some flexibility for green features in the overall floor area (Development Bureau [DevB], 2011). These rules could be expanded to include rooftop gardens and green walls, with independent checks to make sure they are maintained and stay healthy after the buildings are finished. Developers who go beyond the green requirements can keep their floor area bonuses, and early consultation with residents and community groups can help ensure the designs meet the needs of families, seniors, and children, not just look good on paper. Importantly, this approach doesn’t mean building fewer homes; it means each building should have its own green spaces, on rooftops, walls, and ground level, to help keep the area cooler.

The primary beneficiaries are low-income public housing residents, elderly citizens and children, who spend the most time outdoors and are least able to rely on air conditioning at home. A greener estate means accessible shade, lower outdoor temperatures and healthier daily walking routes, alongside the new units the city urgently needs. The city also gains long-term climate resilience and reduced heat-related strain on public health services.

The next practical step anyone can take today is to be specific, not just symbolic. When the Housing Authority or Town Planning Board asks for public feedback on a nearby housing project, send in a written comment urging them to include rooftop gardens, green walls, and small pocket parks in the plans. Let them know that residents care about having both good homes and shaded outdoor spaces. One comment can show officials that it’s possible to have both, helping to move beyond the old idea that you have to choose between development and greenery.

Housing and green spaces don’t have to be at odds. Hong Kong can create safe, comfortable homes while also providing cool, green areas, if planners focus on integrating nature into their designs rather than forcing a choice between the two. A city that protects its residents should also protect the trees that help keep everyone cool.
