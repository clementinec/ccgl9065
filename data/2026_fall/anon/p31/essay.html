From a Pop-Up to a Habit

From a Pop-Up to a Habit: Rethinking Clothing Reuse

The shirt was still folded when I found it, tag half torn. I purchased this shirt one year ago for one event and only wore it once. Now it was a problem to be solved because the dress is so big that it fills a quarter of the suitcase. When I need to move out, I have no way to take it with me. I began to consider clothing swap. After browsing the relevant information, I noticed that HKU held a Clothing Swap Pop-up Market on 4 and 5 October 2023.Those dates raise a question on my mind. If a student realized the following week that they needed a jacket or if a student like me who need such an event found that the recent event was years ago. This was not necessarily HKU‘s only clothing swap event, but the gap matters. There is a difference between pop-up event and regular event.

I used to locate climate responsibility inside my own head. I asked myself whether to buy before purchase. I rarely asked the harder question such as if the product is not new, what other option is just as convenient? Restraint was my whole strategy and my answer depended entirely on willpower.

The third week's case study on denim fabric demonstrates that the story of clothing does not begin at the point of purchase. In fact, it involves cotton fields, dyeing factories, cargo ships, and the ownership stage. If we only consider the destination of clothing, we cannot understand its complete history. Research shows that compared to recycling, reusing clothes can increase environmental benefits. Most of the benefits of reusing clothes come from not producing new products (Sandin & Peters, 2018). Collecting 100 garments for reuse can reduce the global warming impact of a cotton T-shirt by 14% (Farrant et al., 2010). When buying second-hand clothes, the climate impact can be reduced by up to 42% each time they are worn (Klooster et al., 2024). This reframed my drawer. Cleaning out the drawer responsibly and buying new clothes did not shrink my carbon footprint. Only by allowing the shirts to continue circulating could the footprint potentially be reduced. Although the campus pilot program was small, it still managed to avoid some of the burdens associated with producing new clothes.

Week 6 explains why willpower alone fails. People choose sustainable options when they are convenient and predictable. Even eco-minded students rarely share or swap clothes without an effortless setup, otherwise defaulting back to shopping apps (Hobson & O'Byrne, 2024). Thus, I stopped asking how to be a more disciplined consumer and started asking who can reshape the conditions for our next choice.

Here is a proposal, a monthly campus clothing swap pilot even on a small scale could make a real difference. Student clubs can simply book a regular time and spot, so everyone knows where to go. To keep things running smoothly without getting overly complicated, a few basic ground rules would help. For starters, we need to pay attention to fabric quality since blended fibers are notoriously tough to recycle down the line (Chen et al., 2021). Beyond that, anyone can grab something they like even if they didn't bring anything to swap, and lining up a local charity beforehand takes care of whatever's left over at the end.

I will not pretend this solves climate change. The exchange room cannot reform the supply chain, and the number of participants also fails to demonstrate any changes in emissions. The clothing exchange process may also encounter some real conflicts, such as concerns about hygiene, mismatches in sizes, and logistics issues regarding the transportation of clothes between individuals (Armouch et al., 2024; Lang & Zhang, 2019). Therefore, the pilot must measure whether swaps truly replace new purchases rather than merely adding clutter. The reason is that estimates suggest every five second-hand acquisitions replace only about three new purchases (Sandin & Peters, 2018; Gaeta & Silvestri, 2026).

This shirt has been sent away by me now. I posted its details online and gave it to a friend of mine because she told me she needed this one to participate in the same one-time event as the one I bought before. The goal is not to turn every day into a market. It is to build a campus where the sustainable choice is the easy one.
