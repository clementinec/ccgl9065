No Border Can Contain the Smoke

Why Indonesia’s Karhutla Crisis Demands Prevention, Not Just Firefighting

Growing up in Indonesia, the dry season felt normal, until you realize what comes with it. Since

July 2026, alongside the dry season, severe forest and land fires (karhutla) have spread across

Sumatra and Kalimantan, choking not only Indonesian communities but also Singapore and

Malaysia. By mid-November, estimated carbon emissions approached 200 million tonnes

(Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service, 2026), and toxic haze caused an estimated 3,560

air-pollution deaths in Indonesia in September alone (ABC News, 2026).

Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (2026) reported that Indonesia's September 2026

fire emissions were the third-highest on record, surpassed only by 2015 and 2019. Despite past

restoration efforts, recurring fires remain a severe regional public health crisis as smoke travels

hundreds of kilometres (Grosvenor et al., 2024). SCMP’s Asia desk (2026) reported welfare loss

of approximately US$1.97 billion in Malaysia from the 2026 karhutla, further contributing to an

estimated 1,700 deaths. Thus, what begins as a land-management problem in Indonesia ends up

as a global climate challenge.

The severity of Indonesia's forest and land fires (karhutla) crisis is closely connected to the

degradation of tropical peatlands. While peatlands help lock away enormous amounts of carbon,

deforestation and drainage for agricultural expansion make them increasingly vulnerable to

burning. Additionally, although El Niño-related drought intensifies fire seasons, human activities

create the underlying conditions for these disasters (Kiely et al., 2021).

As suggested by Hasan et al. (2026), to solve this issue, Indonesia must shift from reactive

firefighting towards preventive peatland management and strict penalties for illegal land

clearing. Therefore, I propose a strategy built around three priorities: peatland restoration,

community participation, and corporate accountability.

First, the government should prioritize restoring degraded peatlands through rewetting. Existing

canals that were dug to drain peatlands for agricultural purposes (drainage canals) lower

groundwater levels, allowing peat to dry and become highly flammable. Blocking these canals

can restore water levels and reduce fire risk. Kiely et al. (2021) support this, estimating that

completing the planned restoration of 2.49 million hectares of degraded peatland could have

reduced the 2015 burned area by 6%, CO₂ emissions by 18%, and PM₂.₅ emissions by 24%.

However, it is important that restoration considers existing land rights and the livelihoods of

communities dependent on these landscapes.

Second, local communities must become active participants in fire prevention. Government-led

enforcement alone cannot effectively monitor every vulnerable area. Communities should

receive training, equipment, and financial support to establish village-level fire monitoring and

prevention teams. They should also have access to sustainable agricultural alternatives that

reduce dependence on burning for land preparation. Carmenta et al. (2021) demonstrate that

successful fire prevention depends on combinations of interventions, including sanctions and

community awareness, rather than relying on a single measure, reflecting a systems-thinking

approach, where environmental protection cannot succeed without considering the economic

circumstances of those living within affected landscapes.

Third, Indonesia must strengthen corporate accountability. Companies operating in agriculture

and forestry should be required to demonstrate traceability throughout their supply chains and

face significant punishments for illegal burning and land degradation. Governments should

enforce existing environmental regulations more strictly, while international buyers should

prioritise suppliers with verified sustainable practices. Global demand for agricultural

commodities should not come at the expense of irreplaceable ecosystems.

These measures would not only benefit Indonesia, but others as well. Whereas local communities

would experience cleaner air, fewer health risks, and more secure livelihoods, neighboring

countries would benefit from reduced greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution. Kiely et al.

(2021) estimated that peatland restoration could have generated US$8.4 billion in economic

savings across major Indonesian fire events between 2004 and 2015. Prevention is therefore not

merely an environmental expense but an economically rational investment.

As consumers and members of an interconnected global economy, we can act today. Every

purchase we make sends a signal up the supply chain. So, choose certified sustainable products

when buying everyday groceries, cosmetics, and paper products. Look for sustainability

certifications, such as the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil (RSPO) or Forest Stewardship

Council (FSC). Additionally, support community-led peatland restoration projects.

We must stop treating forest fires as inevitable and start treating their prevention as a shared

responsibility. The smoke of karhutla may feel miles away, yet our daily choices either feed the

fire or help put it out. Remember, no border can contain the smoke. Protect the peatlands, protect

the air, protect the planet.
