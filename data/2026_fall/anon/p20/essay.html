One City. Two Climates.

Inside the library of The University of Hong Kong (HKU), it is easy to find students wearing hoodies or jackets even in the summer. In contrast, a subdivided flat in Kwun Tong with no air conditioner reached 39°C in July 2025 (Hong Kong Free Press, 2025). Paradoxically, people in Hong Kong are too cold and too hot in the same city. In Hong Kong, buildings use about 90 percent of the city’s electricity (HKSAR Government, 2024), and most of this electricity is produced by burning natural gas and coal (HKSAR Government, 2026). The average temperature has been rising for decades due to human activities that release tons of carbon emissions. The temperature rise not only creates higher demand for air conditioners but also requires enormous amounts of fuel for energy production. This trend is likely to continue, and the energy demand from air conditioners worldwide is expected to triple by 2050 (International Energy Agency, 2018). In 2012, the Hong Kong government launched the Energy Saving Charter, which asks buildings to keep the indoor temperature between 24°C and 26°C in summer. In 2026, more than 3,500 premises, including 247 shopping centers, signed it (Earth.Org, 2026). However, malls have the freedom not to follow the pledge. In a 2016 survey, nine out of ten malls that signed the Charter kept their indoor temperature outside the range (Earth.Org, 2026). Since keeping customers is far more important than saving energy for shopping centers (Kwok et al., 2017), they have little reason to follow the commitment. For this reason, it is essential to set a legal limit on the lowest indoor temperature in large commercial buildings. In South Korea, for instance, public institutions are legally required to keep the indoor temperature at 28°C or above in summer (Jun et al., 2020). Compared to South Korea, a 24°C limit would be quite a generous policy that would not face a fierce backlash from the public. Only a simple amendment to the existing building energy law would be needed to make this policy real.

The new rule should start with the buildings that have already agreed to the Charter. Inspections would be held regularly, especially during the summer. If a building breaks the rule, it would be charged a penalty, and the money could be used for the public good, such as helping low-income families pay their summer electricity bills. According to CLP Power, raising the temperature by 1°C reduces electricity use by 3 percent (Earth.Org, 2026). Mall operators may argue that shoppers like it cold. Kwok et al. (2017) studied shopping malls in Hong Kong and found that most visitors were satisfied with the temperature, and that the malls were not as cold as people believed. However, the same study found that the voluntary agreement on temperature was working poorly. Moreover, 24°C is the lowest point of the 24 - 26°C range that the government itself recommends, so a law would not ask for anything new. And if every mall follow the same rule, no mall would lose customers to a colder one. Consequently, this policy would benefit three different groups. First, building owners would pay lower electricity bills. Second, with less electricity use, the city would release less carbon and less waste heat, which eases the urban heat island effect. Lastly, low-income residents could get support for their cooling costs, which improves their quality of living. Without this policy, those who are financially stable would keep overcooling their rooms, while those with limited financial means would keep suffering from the heat. There is something readers can do today. Set your own air conditioner to 25°C. Also, when a mall or a classroom feels too cold, send one email to the Environment and Ecology Bureau and ask them to make the 24°C minimum mandatory. Students in the HKU library should not need hoodies in summer, and a family in Kwun Tong should not have to sleep in a 39°C room. Hong Kong already has enough cooling. It just needs a rule to share it fairly.
