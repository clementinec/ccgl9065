Climate Media Literacy Programme

Every time I had a biomedical lab, I forgot to bring my lab coat. My professors repeatedly warned us that we would have to rent a disposable one for $10 if we forgot. I never remembered. During one biochemistry lab, my professor reminded us, but stated the reason like this: these disposable coats were made of plastic, so it takes thousands of years to degrade. She smiled and slowly added, "And it will harm sea turtles." Sometimes, one remembers something for a very long time because this thing has an inexplicable interesting anchor point for our memory. I do not know why, but that scene stayed with me. Since then, I never forgot to bring my lab coat. I think this is what a good pubic advocation should be like, changes are not motivated by moral shame, but people receiving vivid and persuasive opinions could conduct the sustainable behaviors in a easy and automatic way.

Climate change has long been surrounded by public controversy. The IPCC (2021) concluded that human influence on the warming of the atmosphere, ocean and land is unequivocal. Yet misleading communication can make this evidence seems uncertain. For example, some claims selectively talks about short-term weather variability while ignoring long-term climate trend. Furthermore, people's existing worldviews and political or economic beliefs can influence how they interpret climate information (Lewandowsky, 2021). Like me not recognizing the drawbacks of disposable coats on environments, if the public do not realize how their daily actions have already affected the climate, it’s not necessary to discuss any further adjustments.

The Hong Kong Observatory projects that annual maximum daily rainfall in 2081–2100 could be 28.1% higher than the 1995–2014 average under a very high emissions scenario (Hong Kong Observatory, n.d.). Our semester’s topics also connect: food and clothing depend on resource-intensive supply chains; buildings shape energy demand; and the oceans are affected by warming and pollution. What climate communication should do is to connect these systems to choices about consumption, energy and urban planning.

My experience has illustrated the concept of framing: the way information is selected and presented influences how people interpret an issue (Entman, 1993). We understood the importance of framing and might want to train young people’s critical analysis skills on certain claims. Therefore, a Climate Media Literacy Programme in Hong Kong schools and universities might raise awareness, where students construct their own frame by looking at some raw data, identify misleading framing, and learn to recruit and perform public engagement. Sometimes, action comes naturally after people have a deeper understanding of what climate change is about and what are the relationships of us with it.

Another useful strategy we can use in public advocation is that, people from different fields talk about their own experiences. Scientists can explain evidence, while local doctors, engineers, teachers, fishers and community workers connect climate risks to health, buildings, livelihoods and the sea. Effects of climate change that have infiltrated into their life are going to provide stronger perspectives to those who feel less interested. Public sessions should welcome questions about uncertainty, costs and policy trade-offs, rather than treating every concern as denial. Climate communication research recommends clear, repeated messages from a range of trusted and caring communicators (Maibach et al., 2023). A 2026 study further identifies clarity, sincerity and shared values as factors associated with trust in climate communicators (MacInnes et al., 2026).

Some people may say that this proposal has limits. Deep distrust are harder to be removed that we thought, and communication may have to combine with replace emissions cuts, resilient infrastructure or fair climate policy to achieve realistic progress. In a 12-country study of 6,816 participants, Spampatti et al. (2024) found almost no evidence that six inoculation strategies protected people from climate disinformation. No single lesson can create permanent “immunity”. The programme should therefore be evaluated over time, adapted to different audiences, collaborate transparent institutions, and enriched with accessible local evidence.

In my opinion, Hong Kong needs citizens who can question misleading posts, listen to different perspectives, discuss genuine trade-offs and conduct collective actions. The goal is to help them think critically, and act in an easier and evidential way.
