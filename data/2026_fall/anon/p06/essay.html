Cooling Hong Kong in a Warming World

Cooling Hong Kong in a Warming World In 2024, Agence France-Presse reported 84 year old retired janitor Chun Loi sweating in her windowless, 50 sqft Hong Kong home despite running two fans. Her story shows how difficult escaping the heat can be when someone lives in a small, poorly ventilated flat. As temperatures rise, keeping people safe depends on the homes and neighbourhoods they live in. Climate change is heating our planet, and 2024 was Earth’s hottest year on record according to CNN. Moreover, in dense cities like Hong Kong, urban design amplifies the problem even further. Concrete buildings and roads absorb heat throughout the day and release it at night. Meanwhile, closely packed towers can block cooling breezes, and limited greenery means less shade and cooling through evaporation. Together, these factors create the phenomena known as the urban heat island, where urban areas remain warmer than nearby areas, especially at night. In 2024 alone, the Hong Kong Observatory reported 50 hot nights, where the temperature never dropped below 28 degrees on those nights. Not only does this heat make daily life uncomfortable, it can also threaten people’s health, as they give the body less chance to recover. A 2020 report by CUHK linked hot nights to a 2.4% higher risk of death, and up to 6.7% for five consecutive hot nights. Outdoor workers, older people, those with existing illnesses and residents of poorly ventilated homes are particularly vulnerable as their bodies face greater stress. Here’s the catch: the hotter our cities get, the more electricity we use for air conditioning. When fossil fuels supply that electricity, the resulting emissions contribute to further global warming, creating a vicious cycle of rising heat & cooling demand. Therefore, we need to break this cycle before our cities become even more dangerous to live in. Hong Kong should focus on two connected priorities: making buildings and streets cooler, and reducing emissions from the energy we use. That starts with improving the places where heat already builds up. The government should fund retrofits to older housing, schools, and care homes. External window shades can block sunlight before it heats rooms, while reflective roofs absorb less solar energy. Developers should also demonstrate how new buildings allow airflow and limit overheating before projects receive approval. The same approach should also extend to public spaces. Transport and planning authorities should provide shade at exposed bus stops and along busy walking routes. Medellín, Colombia shows what shading can achieve. It added greenery along 18 roads and 12 waterways & city officials reported temperature reductions of more than 2°C (UNEP, 2019). Hong Kong

could similarly plant suitable trees along existing streets, then measure the cooling effects before expanding. However, even with better urban design, people will still need air conditioning. This brings us to our second goal of reducing emissions from electricity use. The International Energy Agency (2024) reports that highly efficient air conditioning units can use half as much electricity as average models. The government should therefore subsidize replacement of inefficient units for low income households, helping them stay cool while using and spending less on electricity. Where that electricity comes from matters too. The government and power companies should invest more in renewable energy, such as solar & wind, and work more with neighbouring regions to secure more renewable electricity. Regional cooperation is particularly important because Hong Kong’s limited space constrains local generation (Environment Bureau, 2021). Doing so would reduce Hong Kong’s reliance on fossil fuels and lower emissions from air conditioning and other everyday activities, helping Hong Kong meet its energy needs while playing its part in combating global warming.

Striving for change is one thing, but making these improvements brings practical challenges, as they require upfront spending and regular maintenance. Therefore, to manage costs, the government should introduce cooling upgrades gradually and prioritize neighbourhoods most exposed to heat. Residents can support these changes as well through simple everyday choices. Closing blinds before direct sunlight enters can reduce cooling demand, while taking public transport can help cut emissions. Students can also join campus greening projects and reduce wasted electricity by switching off lights and air conditioning when study rooms are no longer in use. For residents like Chun, these improvements could mean a safer home that costs less to keep cool. By creating cooler neighbourhoods and reducing the emissions that drive warming worldwide, Hong Kong can play its part in tackling climate change while protecting people from the heat they already face.
