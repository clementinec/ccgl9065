Save Our Sea or Swallow Our Waste

Blue Economy and Marine Ecosystem: From Exploitation to Protection

Mr. Wong has worked in fishery for decades. He reads the sea like his old friend, knowing every wave and tide. But on one day, when he picked up his nets, his hands froze. There were fewer and stunted fish, with single-use plastics and industrial pollutants. He felt like the tide turned against everything he knew.

Mr. Wong’s empty net is not an isolated misfortune. This mirrors a global pattern. Marine ecosystems are facing severe threats from climate change. Ocean warming destroys stability of capture fisheries. Global production of tuna and swordfish is estimated to decrease by 36%, with fish size reduction by 15% by 2050 (ErauskinExtramiana et al., 2023). These declines illustrate the significance of mitigating climate change to sustain and meet market demand.

Hong Kong’s water also tells the same story. The fishing industry in Hong Kong yielded around 90460 tonnes of fishery produce by over 10000 local fishermen in 2025, offering stable marine supply to consumers (Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation

Department, 2026), illustrating the need for supporting local fisheries to promote food and job security.

For traditional fishers like Mr. Wong, who rely on ocean to make a living, the deteriorating marine ecosystem should be addressed with proactive marine protection to save coastal communities, fishing industry and the ocean. Therefore, I propose a 3step strategy of community-led marine protected areas (MPAs) that protect, clean and shield the waters.

The first step is expanding MPAs beyond current coverage. Hong Kong’s marine protected coverage has risen from 2% to 6% (WWF Hong Kong, 2026) but is far from the target of 20% in the government’s Five-Year Plan (HKSAR, 2026), showing insufficient protection of marine environment. Evidence shows why small area is not enough. Using Philippines as an example, after MPA implementation on Balicasag, Balicasag sanctuary increased living hard coral cover by 119% from 1984 to 1999 (Christie et al., 2002), showing higher biodiversity and improved marine environment. However, when the committee was replaced by tourism authority, fish stocks dropped. Therefore, Hong Kong government should establish community-led MPAs with a co-

management model. The Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation Department could collaborate with fishers to manage MPA. Fishers could become guardians of the fisheries and protect the fisheries they depend on.

The second step focuses on stricter waste regulations. Zhang et al. (2026) conducted a study and found that small microplastics in Hong Kong coastal waters are at average concentration of 362 particles/m³, which is 100 times higher than large microplastics collected. The most abundant polymer is polyethylene terephthalate from machinewashed materials. Protection means little when pollution continues. Thus, Hong Kong should implement stricter waste controls, including making microplastic filters in commercial laundry and municipal wastewater plants mandatory and phasing out single-use plastics. Hence, microplastics are kept out of water, protecting not only marine species but also consumers who eat seafood.

The third step is coastal habitat restoration. Healthy coral reefs reduce wave energy by 97% (Ferrario et al., 2014) but the bleached and degraded ones lose this function. Consequently, Hong Kong’s marine resilience is lowered, more prone to extreme weather events. This city has demonstrated the success of restoration. In Tolo Harbour,

1000 coral fragments are transplanted, achieving 85% survival rate. In Hoi Ha Wan, 3D-printed reef tiles provide habitat for marine species. Mangroves in Mai Po likely protected Fairview Park from flooding during super-typhoon Mangkhut. As a result, Hong Kong should expand mangrove, coral and seagrass restoration to rebuild wavedissipation capacity that warming has destroyed. Hence, coastal and marine environment could be better protected.

This comprehensive solution benefits diverse stakeholders. Indeed, mrarine environment is protected from external human damage. By expanding co-managed MPAs, fishers could be financially stable with protected fish stocks. Families living in coastal area would be less prone to severe weather events under wave-dissipation by species. With waste regulations, seafood consumers could eat safer foods without microplastics. Lastly, the next generation who inherits current marine environment would be protected.

Readers can show support by avoiding seafood listed on IUCN Red List, asking restaurants where their stocks come from, and expressing opinion on adopting sustainable purchasing. Every meal affects the ocean future, and you can make the

choice.

The tide that turned against Mr. Wong’s thinking was not only owing to nature. It was also traceable to human choices. But we can turn it back by choosing differently. MPA is not a fence but a promise that fish we take today will still be there tomorrow.
