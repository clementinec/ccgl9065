When Colors Disappear: Saving Hong Kong’s Coral Reefs

What happens when the ocean that once sustained generations becomes a world their children may only know through photographs? Guanzon grew up in a coastal town in the Philippines. He remembers going out with his father on fishing trips: the boat surrounded by schools of fish so rich that the shallow water grew dark. “When I dove in the ocean, it was even more beautiful”, he claimed, full of regret. Beneath the surface were corals stretching across the seabed, screaming in colors. It was an upside-down world of extraordinary beauty. The ocean was far beyond a workplace; it was his friend, childhood, and livelihood. However, when he returned to those same waters years later, there were “almost no fish”, and patches of coral turned ghostly white. The ocean looked dead. The world that bred his childhood was disappearing in front of him.

Although his story comes from the Philippines, the threat is close to home. Hong Kong, especially in the eastern and northeastern waters around Hoi-Ha-Wan and Yan-Chau-Tong, nurtures 84 species of abundant hard corals (AFCD, 2025), and these homes are increasingly threatened by rising sea temperatures. The temperatures cause the once vibrant corals to starve, expel the microscopic algae that provide their energy, turn completely white, and potentially die due to coral bleaching. In 2022, research regarding Hong Kong’s marine heatwaves reported that the proportion of bleached coral cover across nine surveyed sites ranged from 2.4% to an alarming 70.3% (Chung et al., 2024). Such large-scale local coral bleaching was also recorded by AFCD, where 13 sites in its 2024 Reef Check experienced bleaching associated with prolonged elevated sea temperatures (AFCD, 2025a). This crisis extends beyond Hong Kong and coral reefs alone. The United Nations reported in 2021 that approximately 14% of coral was lost between 2009 and 2018, while coral reefs support at least 25% of marine species (UNEP, 2021). Their disappearance marks a threatening reality to marine biodiversity that devastates the ecological balance of coastal waters and local communities. Hong Kong cannot afford delaying this environmental problem.

To tackle this problem, I propose establishing a Hong Kong Coral Resilience Partnership. We should no longer rely on emergency responses only after we observe bleaching. Instead, we should unite the government, universities, and local fishing communities to protect our remaining corals. This partnership aims to detect damage before it occurs, identify resilient local coral populations, and restore habitats. First, HK should promote stronger scientific monitoring. AFCD, in its 2025 Reef Check program, surveyed 36 sites with more than 1,300 volunteer divers and concluded that a more comprehensive early-warning network should be developed. Monitoring systems, such as underwater temperature sensors and frequent coral-health checks, are necessary to locate vulnerable locations before bleaching. Through these, climate refugia can also be identified, which are areas where more resilient corals experience less heat stress. Scientists can focus on restoring these locations, while more damaged reefs should be restored only where conditions allow recovery. Indeed, at CUHK, researchers have already established deeper-water nurseries to cultivate stress-tolerant corals for potential transplantation. Alongside governmental support, these new technologies can empower a sustainable scientific monitoring system to cultivate a more robust coral community.

Second, restoring corals isn’t effective if damage continues. HK should strengthen ecological protection by valuing biodiverse areas, including Hoi-Ha-Wan and Yan-Chau-Tong. The government also plays an irreplaceable role in coral preservation. Governments should reduce polluted runoff and prevent destructive fishing to restrict activities destroying corals, thereby reducing avoidable local pressures. Above all, as an important step in active conservation, local communities mustn’t be left to carry the burden of restoration alone. Fishers should receive paid training and government-supported employment in reef monitoring. Financial support should also be long-term and available if environmental restrictions affect local business income. Protecting the ocean should never mean sacrificing families.

The responsibility lies greatly on us, too. We should choose responsibly sourced seafood, reduce plastic waste, and support local conservation initiatives to help reduce pressure on marine ecosystems through simple actions. We should demand that marine biodiversity receive actual consideration in Hong Kong’s environmental policies. We cannot stop global warming alone, but we can give vulnerable coral communities a better chance of surviving it. In 2025, Coral Beach in Hoi-Ha-Wan reported an 84.7% coral cover (AFCD, 2025b). This reminds us that Hong Kong still has vibrant marine ecosystems to protect.

One day, our children may ask what Hong Kong’s ocean once looked like. We want our answer to not simply be a photograph of what we failed to save.
