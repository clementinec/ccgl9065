Reconnect with Your Closet

Reconnect with your closet - end fast fashion from within!

I own almost 200 items of clothing. Over the last month I wore roughly 40 of them (if I’m being generous). As I cleaned out my closet, I uncovered over 50 pieces of clothing that I hadn’t touched in months, and five I couldn’t remember having worn at all. One was a crimson skirt stuffed in the back of my closet, tag still on, in a color I didn’t even know I owned. Had I become so caught up in following trends that I stopped paying attention to what I buy?

Standing in front of my closet, I wondered how different things could have been if I simply noticed the skirt and wore it more. WRAP, a UK-based charity, found that extending the active life of a piece of UK clothing by 9 months could reduce carbon, waste and water footprints by up to 20% (Let’s recycle, 2017). In less than a year, I could’ve actually created a positive impact, but instead I kept shopping. I realise now that the problem wasn't that I had nothing to wear, it was that I wasn't wearing what I already had.

One article argued that fast fashion thrives on a constant influx of new stock that creates a sense of exclusivity, pushing people to buy things they don’t need. Mango, for example, has been releasing new products every two weeks since February 2016 (Sustainable brands, 2018). Reading this made me realise that somewhere between the sales and new arrivals, I had stopped asking myself if I needed any of it. To me, this felt connected back to what we learned in class about incentives; people don’t act in a vacuum, they respond to the system around them. I didn’t buy the skirt because I’m careless, I bought it because I saw a good deal.

This isn’t isolated to just my closet. In Hong Kong, a 2017 independent survey discovered that out of 1000 local residents, 4 in 10 people discarded clothing after only wearing it once (South China Morning Post, 2018). Read that again, once. Hong Kong alone sent an average of 388 tonnes of textiles to the city’s overflowing landfills every day and about half of that was estimated to be clothing (South China Morning Post, 2018). Behind each of these tonnes is the energy and water it took to make the clothes in the first place, only for them to be worn briefly and be discarded (Ellen MacArthur Foundation, 2017).

In Hong Kong, second-hand clothing has recently gained traction amongst younger people. Students are ditching malls for small second-hand stores in places like Mong Kok searching for hidden treasures they consider to be retro and vintage (Shroffed, 2026). However, a psychological barrier remains with one HKU student stating she preferred new clothes because they feel “cleaner and more comfortable” (Lifestyle.inq, 2018). In today’s fast-fashion age, our brains have been trained to see new as better and old as lesser.

The solution I want to focus on is simple: it is to keep clothes in use for longer by creating a culture of swapping and exchanging. Rather than waiting for the fashion industry giants to change their ways, we can work to build a more circular economy ourselves (Ellen MacArthur Foundation, 2017). Take out plastic’s (a local charity) TOP Swop events let people exchange clothing in a collective, shared space and so reduce the sense of taboo associated with secondhand clothing.

Yes, swapping does mean giving up the thrill of buying something new, but at the same time hunting for a hidden find on a swap table can be just as fun, and nobody has to pay for it, not even the environment! Even though swaps can’t fix the whole system, they can help to keep clothes in use longer, which ties back to WRAP’s research points (Let’s Recycle, 2017). There’s no need for some big authority or council to get involved. Just ask ten friends to bring three items that they no longer find themselves wearing, and bring a needle and thread for the rest.

Sifting through my closet showed me that the problem wasn’t that I needed more space but rather that I needed to value what I already owned. The crimson skirt is still at the back, and I’m not throwing it away. Instead, I’m going to take it with me to my first swap, and I hope someone finally wears it.
