Publish the Figure Beyond the Boundary

The uncounted emissions beyond the boundary

Living in Hong Kong, my life is full of imported goods, like electronics from America, beef from Australia and clothes from China. They are produced outside Hong Kong, producing CO2 somewhere else. Hong Kong residents enjoy them while being proud of low emission of CO2 in Hong Kong, 4.35 tonnes in 2024 (Our World in Data, n.d.-a), less than a third of that in the US. If all the emissions produced by the products we consume were really counted back to us, this figure would become 19.9 tonnes (Our World in Data, n.d.-b), even higher than the US’s. Our lifestyle would seem no longer environmentally friendly.

This is not because the government lies to us. According to the rule of UNFCCC (IPCC, 2006), the government should count the fuel that burns inside the country only so that emissions will not be double counted. It flatters Hong Kong where 61% of the emissions is from electricity, 18% from transport and 8% from waste and almost nothing from manufacturing as the factories are elsewhere (Environment and Ecology Bureau, 2026). And researchers in Hong Kong estimate that 62% consumption-based emissions here comes from imports (Yau et al., 2018).

This means Hong Kong’s emission figure is technically accurate but materially misleading as a large amount of the emissions which support our life occurs beyond our territorial boundary. When Hong Kong celebrates the low emission in Hong Kong, the pollution related to the consumption is experienced by communities beyond its border.

So I think the solution is publishing the two figures in the same year in the same format. One figure is calculated in the previous way the government did in the past, while the other one should be based on consumption. They work together to make a comparison, showing that importing more is just a cheap but not the best way to make Hong Kong greener. And a new standard of progress in environment protection would be shrinking the gap between the two figures. This method has zero political cost as no one is forced to reduce the emissions but can help more people realize the hidden carbon account behind everyday consumption of imports.

This method has had precedents in the UK. Defra has published the UK’s carbon footprint with the territorial emissions every year since 2012. According to the figures, UK territorial emissions fell 50% between 1996 and 2023, while its footprint fell 15%, and the share of its footprint from imports rose from 31% to 53% (Defra, 2026). The second figure punctures the environmentally-friendly situation shown by the first one. But there may be a problem that the consumption-based estimates are unstable, as the re-exports can be counted twice. Defra’s response to this objection is publishing its assumptions and revisions. Hong Kong could do the same thing. An imperfect estimate is better than nothing.

If Hong Kong government publishes these two figures in the same year, its standing as a green city would change. Hong Kong looked clean partly because the emissions happen somewhere else. This fact sounds uncomfortable but it should be accepted, as Hong Kong promised to achieve carbon neutrality by 2050, with an absolute cut against 2005 meanwhile (Environment and Ecology Bureau, n.d.). Only the use of consumer-side accounting can show Hong Kong has really reduced carbon dioxide emissions instead of transferring emissions to other places. And this will benefit and give an example to other port cities like Singapore, Rotterdam and Dubai if Hong Kong becomes the first to publish both figures.

For us, except keeping the green and environmentally friendly lifestyle, we can start a small and specific action today. According to the Code on Access to Information (Government of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region, n.d.), any member of the public can ask a government bureau for the information it holds. One request to the Environment and Ecology Bureau for a consumption-based estimate figure covering the same year is enough to draw attention. The government may still consider whether to release the data, but each request makes the next one harder to refuse.

Before taking this course, I did not realize that where the boundary draw will changes the answer, and accurate facts can also be misleading. I still believe the figure 4.35 tonnes published by the government, but before I become proud of this figure, I will consider the cost it brings to other regions.
