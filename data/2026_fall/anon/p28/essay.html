There Is No Planet B

CCGL9065 Our Response to Climate Change: Hong Kong 2100 Final Reflective Essay

The fishing net that came up with empty of fish

Imagine yourself as a fisherman. On a humid morning, you hauled his net and found more plastics stuck in it than fishes. Bottles, food wrappers, fragments of white styrofoam from oyster rafts and tangled fishing lines filled the mesh. You sorted the catch by hand, tossing everything you caught back into the sea that once fed your family. That quiet moment is no longer rare along Hong Kong’s coastline. The food, textiles, and industry pollution we’ve produced have eventually flowed into the sea, causing irreversible damage to our ocean. The Ocean absorbs 29-30% of the carbon dioxide we emit from daily life. (UN News, 2026; Global Carbon Project, 2025). Climate change has reduced the ocean carbon absorption capacity, warmer surface water reduces solubility and lowers Ocean PH which harms shellbuilding marine life and ecosystems. At the same time, we are also loading the ocean with debris. Microplastics produced by humans also appeared at several coastal sites in Hong Kong, with concentration highest at river mouths and near urban discharges. (Lo et al.,2025). Over 60 percent of marine litter collected on local shores originated from land-based activities, and citizens in Hong Kong use approximately 5million plastic bottles everyday which further intensified ocean pollution.(Lee et al., 2024) These numbers are not abstract. Across the semester we saw how different systems create these pressures: Everyday convenient food packaging, takeaway containers, and aquaculture floats enters drains and rivers; types of particles and road dusts washed off streets during rain, synthetic microfibres from fabric escape from washing machine and enters the sewage systems, industrial production, energy systems and transport that generated particles and carbon emissions. Once these pollutants enter the water, they do not disappear. They entangle and choke ocean animals, damaging marine life habitats. When we pollute the ocean, we are not only dirtying the water; we are damaging the living systems that sustain fisheries, coastal protection, and the climate itself.The ocean sits at the end of these chains. What begins as a packaging decision or a fast-fashion purchase ends in our fishing net. Stopping the flow of plastic and waste is an act of protecting the ocean’s ability to protect us. A concrete response already exists. Strengthening mandatory extended producer responsibility would require companies that place packaging and bottles on the market to finance collection, recycling and redesign. (GovHK,2026; Environment Bureau, 2021) This shifts the cost upstream, motivating producers to create incentives for less wasteful designs. We citizens also hold the mighty power; The reusable water bottle we carry, the restaurants

that use less packaging or the takeaway containers we refuse to take, the microfibre-catching laundry bags we use ,fast fashion purchases we avoid when possible, the volunteering actions like harbour clean up we join can significantly lower the environmental burden. These actions matter urgently because everyday delay will allow more plastic to accumulate in the ocean. As larger pieces of plastics break down, more microplastics enter the food chain, first into plankton then into big fishes, last it goes back into us. If we humans do not want to consume microplastics and polluted waste, then why should marine life be forced into ? In fact, we depend on the ocean far more than we admit, as it regulates the climate, supports the fishing industry, protects the coastline and sustains the web of life that makes our own survival possible. When we pollute, we are not damaging something abstract, but we are the ones who weaken the ocean system that keeps us alive. Being considerate is not only showing kindness to marine life but also shows a responsibility towards ourselves and towards those who might bear the consequences after us. However, change does not always start with policies and legislation, it often begins with a quiet decision like refusing to use plastic bags, bottles, containers and initiating climate actions like collecting litter that would otherwise drift out to the sea. We all know the ways to combat climate change, yet our laziness always hinders us from taking our first step. The ocean has carried us for generations, it is the time for us to carry some responsibilities in return. Imagine a different morning in Sai Kung once again. You haul in your net and find fish that move vividly, instead of bottles and broken styrofoam. The weight in the hands feels similar again. Relief mixes with quiet gratitude. This moment is not impossible, and it depends on the choice we make now.
